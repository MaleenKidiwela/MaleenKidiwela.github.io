# Coda-Wave Sensitivity Kernel for a Deep Repeating Source and a Surface Receiver

**S-wave coda monitoring of LFE families (Cascadia, source depth $\approx 30$ km) — forward and inverse problem**

---

## 0. What is actually new here

Classical coda-wave interferometry (CWI) and passive image interferometry build their travel-time sensitivity kernels for a **surface source / surface receiver** geometry, and they treat the coda as a *composite* of a 2-D surface-wave field (early coda) and a 3-D body-wave field (late coda) (Obermann et al., 2013b, 2016; Margerin et al., 2016). That composite is why the standard kernel has two strong lobes *beneath each station* and weak-but-nonzero sensitivity in between, decaying with depth.

Your geometry breaks three of those assumptions, and each break simplifies or relocates the kernel:

1. **The source is deep** ($z_s = d \approx 30$ km), inside the fault zone, and is a *repeating* LFE family — i.e. an effectively fixed point source you can re-record indefinitely.
2. **A deep source does not radiate surface waves.** At $d \gg \lambda$ the source-side field is *pure 3-D body-wave (S) intensity*. There is no 2-D component on the source leg. This is the analytical content of your statement "we clearly have only S in the coda": the source leg is single-mode 3-D, and the only place a 2-D (surface-wave) channel can enter is the free surface near the receiver.
3. **The receiver is at the free surface**, so the receiver leg carries the free-surface energy amplification and is the *only* leg where body↔surface coupling can occur.

The consequence is a kernel with one lobe **pinned in the fault zone** (at the source) and one lobe at the surface station, joined by a sensitivity tube. Because every surface station shares the *same* deep source, the deep lobe is **common-mode across the whole network** — which, as shown in §6 and §9, is exactly what lets you separate a fault-zone change from a shallow site change.

Nobody has written the kernel for this geometry. Below I derive it from radiative-transfer / diffusion first principles, give the explicit half-space closed form, and then formulate both the forward map ($\delta\beta/\beta \to$ coda observables) and the regularized inverse problem (coda observables $\to$ fault-zone $\delta\beta/\beta$ and source-location change).

## 1. Assumptions and notation

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $\mathbf{s}=(0,0,d)$ | source position (LFE family centroid), depth $d$ |
| $\mathbf{r}=(\mathbf{r}_h,0)$ | receiver position, on the free surface $z=0$ |
| $\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_h,z)$ | scatterer / model point, $z\ge 0$ downward |
| $\beta$ | shear-wave (S) speed; single propagating mode |
| $\ell$ | scattering mean free path; $\tau=\ell/\beta$ scattering mean free time |
| $\ell^\*$ | transport mean free path, $\ell^\*=\ell/(1-\langle\cos\theta\rangle)$ |
| $D$ | energy diffusivity, $D=\beta\ell^\*/3$ (3-D) |
| $\sigma_a=1/\tau_i$ | intrinsic absorption rate ($\tau_i=Q_i/\omega$) |
| $t$ | lapse time in the coda (measured from the LFE origin time) |
| $P(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2,t)$ | mean intensity (energy-density) propagator |
| $K(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},t)$ | travel-time sensitivity kernel |
| $\delta\beta/\beta$ | relative shear-velocity perturbation field (the target) |
| $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ | source-location displacement vector between two LFE realizations |

**Working assumptions** (each flagged where it matters):
- Single mode (S body waves), isotropic background, statistically homogeneous scattering — so radiative transfer (RTE) applies and, at lapse times $t\gg\tau$, the diffusion approximation holds.
- Free surface treated as a **perfectly reflecting** boundary for S energy → zero net energy flux through $z=0$ (Neumann condition). This is the leading approximation; a radiation/extrapolation-length boundary condition is the refinement (§5).
- Weak, localized perturbations → first-order (Born / linear) sensitivity.
- Coda windows long compared to the dominant period but short compared to the timescale of medium change.

## 2. The intensity propagator

### Step 1 — Radiative transfer and its diffusion limit

The mean intensity (specific energy density integrated over propagation directions) obeys the scalar radiative transfer equation. For $t\gg\tau$ this reduces to a diffusion equation with absorption (Paasschens, 1997; Margerin et al., 2016):

$$
\frac{\partial P}{\partial t} = D\,\nabla^2 P \;-\; \sigma_a\,P \;+\; \delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)\,\delta(t),
\qquad D=\frac{\beta\,\ell^\*}{3}.
\tag{1}
$$

The infinite-medium Green's function of (1) is

$$
P_\infty(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0,t)=\frac{1}{(4\pi D t)^{3/2}}\,
\exp\!\left[-\frac{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0|^2}{4Dt}\right]e^{-\sigma_a t},
\qquad t>0 .
\tag{2}
$$

> *Dimensional check:* $[D]=\mathrm{m^2/s}$, so $[P_\infty]=\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ (energy density per unit source energy), and $\int P_\infty\,d^3x=e^{-\sigma_a t}$ — energy is conserved up to absorption. ✓

### Step 2 — RTE form for early coda (do not throw the ballistic term away)

The diffusion form (2) is only the multiple-scattering limit. The full RTE intensity carries a coherent **ballistic** term plus a scattered halo (Paasschens, 1997, 3-D scalar, isotropic scattering):

$$
P_{\mathrm{RTE}}(R,t)=\underbrace{\frac{e^{-\beta t/\ell}}{4\pi R^2}\,\delta\!\left(t-\frac{R}{\beta}\right)}_{\text{ballistic}}
\;+\;\underbrace{P_{\mathrm{sc}}(R,t)\,\Theta\!\left(t-\frac{R}{\beta}\right)}_{\text{scattered halo}},\qquad R=|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0|,
\tag{3}
$$

where the halo $P_{\mathrm{sc}}$ smoothly interpolates from single scattering to the Gaussian (2) at large $t$, and $\Theta$ is the Heaviside causality factor. The factor $e^{-\beta t/\ell}$ is the **exponential decay** you referred to: it attenuates intensity along ballistic/low-order paths by the number of mean free paths traversed. Equation (3) is the object to use in the early S-coda; equation (2) is its long-lapse limit. (I keep $P$ generic below; substitute (2) for the closed form and (3) — or a Monte-Carlo RTE solution — for accuracy in the early coda.)

### Step 3 — Half-space images and the free-surface S amplification

With the reflecting free surface, build the half-space propagator by the method of images. Let $\mathbf{x}_0=(\mathbf{x}_{0h},z_0)$ and its mirror $\mathbf{x}_0'=(\mathbf{x}_{0h},-z_0)$. Using $g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0,t)=(4\pi Dt)^{-3/2}\exp[-|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0|^2/4Dt]$ (absorption stripped for clarity, restored by an overall $e^{-\sigma_a t}$):

$$
P_{\!\frac12}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0,t)=
g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0,t)+g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0',t).
\tag{4}
$$

Two important specializations:

- **Source leg** ($\mathbf{x}_0=\mathbf{s}=(0,0,d)$): a genuine two-term propagator — real source at depth $d$ plus image at $-d$. Crucially there is **no surface-wave term**: a source at $d\approx 30\,\mathrm{km}\gg\lambda$ does not excite the 2-D channel. The source leg is strictly 3-D body-wave.

- **Receiver leg** ($\mathbf{x}_0=\mathbf{r}$, $z_0=0$): the two image terms coincide, giving the well-known **factor-2 free-surface amplification** of a diffuse field:

$$
P_{\!\frac12}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r},t)=\frac{2}{(4\pi D t)^{3/2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{|\mathbf{x}_h-\mathbf{r}_h|^2+z^2}{4Dt}\right].
\tag{5}
$$

The direct source→receiver propagator is then ($|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{s}|^2=|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{s}'|^2=r_h^2+d^2$, because the receiver is at $z=0$):

$$
P_{\!\frac12}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},t)=\frac{2}{(4\pi D t)^{3/2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{r_h^2+d^2}{4Dt}\right]e^{-\sigma_a t},
\qquad r_h=|\mathbf{r}_h|.
\tag{6}
$$

## 3. The travel-time sensitivity kernel

### Step 4 — Path-summation derivation

Decompose the coda as a sum over multiply-scattered S trajectories from $\mathbf{s}$ to $\mathbf{r}$ (Snieder, 2006). A trajectory of geometric length $L$ has travel time $T=\int_{\text{path}}\beta^{-1}\,d\ell$. A relative velocity perturbation $\delta\beta/\beta$ localized near $\mathbf{x}$ changes the travel time by

$$
\delta T=\int_{\text{path}}\delta\!\left(\frac1\beta\right)d\ell=-\int_{\text{path}}\frac{\delta\beta}{\beta}\,\frac{d\ell}{\beta}.
\tag{7}
$$

So each unit of *time* the path spends near $\mathbf{x}$ contributes $-(\delta\beta/\beta)$ to the fractional travel-time change. We therefore need the **expected time a $\mathbf{s}\!\to\!\mathbf{r}$ trajectory of total duration $t$ spends in a volume element around $\mathbf{x}$.** By the Markov (bridge) property of the scattered intensity, the probability density of visiting $\mathbf{x}$ at intermediate time $u$ is $P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x},u)\,P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r},t-u)/P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},t)$. Integrating the occupation time over $u$:

$$
\rho(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},t)}\int_0^{t}P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x},u)\,P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r},t-u)\,du .
\tag{8}
$$

### Step 5 — The kernel and the forward relation

The mean relative travel-time change of the coda recorded at lapse time $t$ is the occupation-time-weighted velocity perturbation:

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{\delta\tau(t)}{t}=-\int_{V}K(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},t)\,\frac{\delta\beta(\mathbf{x})}{\beta(\mathbf{x})}\,d^3x\;}
\tag{9}
$$

$$
\boxed{\;K(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},t)=
\frac{\displaystyle\int_0^{t}P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x},u)\,P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r},t-u)\,du}
{\displaystyle\int_V d^3x'\int_0^{t}P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x}',u)\,P(\mathbf{x}',\mathbf{r},t-u)\,du}\;}
\tag{10}
$$

This is the Pacheco & Snieder (2005) kernel, here written for arbitrary source/receiver positions and for whatever $P$ you choose (diffusion (2), or RTE (3)). The minus sign encodes that a velocity *drop* lengthens travel times ($\delta\beta<0\Rightarrow\delta\tau>0$).

**Normalization.** The Chapman–Kolmogorov identity for the (half-space) propagator,
$\int_V P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x},u)P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r},t-u)\,d^3x = P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},t)$,
collapses the denominator of (10) to $t\,P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},t)$:

$$
K(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},t)=\frac{1}{t\,P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},t)}\int_0^{t}P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x},u)\,P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r},t-u)\,du,
\qquad \int_V K\,d^3x=1 .
\tag{11}
$$

Two consequences: (i) for a *homogeneous* perturbation, (9) gives $\delta\tau/t=-\delta\beta/\beta$ (the familiar stretch); (ii) intrinsic absorption contributes $e^{-\sigma_a u}e^{-\sigma_a(t-u)}=e^{-\sigma_a t}$ to *both* numerator and denominator and therefore **cancels** — the kernel *shape* is independent of $Q_i$ (Pacheco & Snieder, 2005). $Q_i$ only controls how far in lapse time you can push before the coda drops below noise.

### Step 6 — Explicit half-space diffusion kernel

Insert (4)–(6) into (11). Let $a\equiv 4D$. Define the horizontal distances $\rho_s=|\mathbf{x}_h|$ (to the epicentre) and $\rho_r=|\mathbf{x}_h-\mathbf{r}_h|$. The source-leg and receiver-leg propagators are

$$
P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x},u)=\frac{1}{(\pi a u)^{3/2}}
\Big[e^{-\frac{\rho_s^2+(z-d)^2}{a u}}+e^{-\frac{\rho_s^2+(z+d)^2}{a u}}\Big],\qquad
P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r},t-u)=\frac{2}{(\pi a (t-u))^{3/2}}\,e^{-\frac{\rho_r^2+z^2}{a (t-u)}} .
\tag{12}
$$

Hence the kernel is the single quadrature

$$
\boxed{\;
K(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},t)=
\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}(t)}\int_0^{t}\frac{du}{[u(t-u)]^{3/2}}
\Big[e^{-\frac{\rho_s^2+(z-d)^2}{a u}}+e^{-\frac{\rho_s^2+(z+d)^2}{a u}}\Big]
e^{-\frac{\rho_r^2+z^2}{a (t-u)}}\;}
\tag{13}
$$

with the normalizer fixed analytically by (6)/(11):

$$
\mathcal{N}(t)=t\,(\pi a)^{3/2}\,P_{\!\frac12}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},t)\Big/2
= t\,\frac{(\pi a)^{3/2}}{(\pi a t)^{3/2}}\,e^{-\frac{r_h^2+d^2}{a t}}
= \frac{e^{-(r_h^2+d^2)/(a t)}}{\sqrt{t}} .
\tag{14}
$$

Equation (13) is evaluated by one 1-D numerical integral per model point — no full wavefield simulation needed. (The $u$-integral has the form of a product of two heat kernels and can also be written via modified Bessel functions, but numerical quadrature is the practical route.)

## 4. Single-scattering kernel — the isochron ellipsoid

For the **early** S-coda, where only a few scattering events have occurred, use the single-scattering (Born) kernel (Pacheco & Snieder, 2006) instead of (13). Substituting the ballistic term of (3) into (10) gives sensitivity concentrated on the locus of constant total path length:

$$
K_{\mathrm{ss}}(\mathbf{x})\;\propto\;
\frac{e^{-(R_s+R_r)/\ell}}{R_s^2\,R_r^2}\;
\delta\!\left(t-\frac{R_s+R_r}{\beta}\right),\qquad
R_s=|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{s}|,\;\; R_r=|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r}| .
\tag{15}
$$

The $\delta$-function is the surface $R_s+R_r=\beta t$: a **prolate ellipsoid with foci at the source and the receiver**, plus a mirror ellipsoid with focus at the image source $\mathbf{s}'$. The factor $e^{-(R_s+R_r)/\ell}$ is again the exponential mean-free-path decay you mentioned. So the kernel evolves with lapse time from a *thin ellipsoidal shell* (early coda, eq. 15) to the *two-lobe diffusive cloud* (late coda, eq. 13). For a deep source the early-coda ellipsoid is tilted and elongated along the $\mathbf{s}$–$\mathbf{r}$ line, threading the full crustal column including the fault zone.

## 5. Validity, and the one caveat that matters for your geometry

The diffusion kernel (13) is exact only where the perturbation sits in a near-isotropic diffuse field — i.e. **beyond about one transport mean free path $\ell^\*$ from both source and receiver**, in the diffusive regime (Margerin et al., 2016; Barajas et al., 2022). Within $\ell^\*$ of a sensor the energy flux is directional and one needs the *specific-intensity* kernel built from the full RTE angular field, not just the energy density.

For your problem this caveat lands favorably and unfavorably in different places:
- **Deep lobe (fault zone, your target):** at $d\approx 30$ km the source region is far from the surface receiver and, for crustal $\ell^\*$ of order tens of km, plausibly near or beyond $\ell^\*$ — so the diffusion kernel is *most trustworthy exactly where you want the answer*. Good.
- **Near the source itself and near the surface station:** within $\ell^\*$ the diffusion form degrades; use the Margerin et al. (2016) specific-intensity kernel or a Monte-Carlo RTE solution there. This is also the only region where receiver-side body↔surface-wave coupling enters (Barajas et al., 2022; Margerin et al., 2019).

**Recommended practice:** use (13) for the bulk/deep sensitivity, splice in an RTE/specific-intensity kernel within $\ell^\*$ of the surface station, and use Monte-Carlo RTE (Paasschens, 1997; Margerin et al., 2016) as the reference solution to calibrate the partition. This is the deep-source analogue of the Obermann et al. (2013b, 2016) surface-wave (2-D) + body-wave (3-D) splicing — except your source leg has **no 2-D term**, so the only splice is at the receiver.

## 6. Geometry of the kernel, and why the deep lobe is special

Reading (13): $K$ is large where *both* legs are large. $P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x},u)$ peaks near the source; $P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r},t-u)$ peaks near the receiver. So:

- **Deep lobe** at the source $\mathbf{s}$ — *inside the LFE fault zone*. This lobe does not exist in the surface–surface geometry; it is the entire point of using deep repeaters.
- **Surface lobe** at the station $\mathbf{r}$, carrying the factor-2 free-surface enhancement (5).
- **Connecting tube** along $\mathbf{s}$–$\mathbf{r}$, with the early-coda ellipsoid (15) as its sharp limit.

**Lapse-time depth migration.** Early lapse times weight the least-scattered (most direct) paths → sensitivity concentrated along the deep tube and the fault zone. As $t$ grows, the field diffuses, the surface lobe broadens laterally, and shallow near-receiver sensitivity gains relative weight. Scanning coda windows $t_1<t_2<\dots$ therefore gives you a *depth-resolving* family of kernels — the lever that lets you attribute a change to the fault zone rather than the shallow crust.

**Common-mode deep lobe (the network trick).** Every surface station shares the *same* source $\mathbf{s}$, so every station's kernel has its deep lobe in the *same* place. A real fault-zone velocity change therefore produces a **coherent $\delta v/v$ across all station pairs and all lapse times**, weighted by the shared deep-lobe sensitivity $\int_{\text{fault}}K\,d^3x$. A shallow change near one station shows up only in that station's pairs. This common-mode-vs-station-specific contrast is a strong, model-light discriminant and should be built directly into the inversion (§9).

## 7. Forward problem (two observables)

For each station $\mathbf{r}_a$ and lapse-time window centered at $t_i$ you measure two things from the coda of repeating LFEs:

**(a) Stretch / mean travel-time change** (velocity-change channel):

$$
\left(\frac{\delta\tau}{\tau}\right)_{a,i}=-\int_V K(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r}_a,t_i)\,\frac{\delta\beta(\mathbf{x})}{\beta(\mathbf{x})}\,d^3x .
\tag{16}
$$

**(b) Waveform decorrelation** (structural-change / scatterer-change channel). A localized change in scattering strength $\delta\Sigma$ (new cracks, fluid fronts) decorrelates the coda with a kernel of the same convolutional form but built from the *single-visit* intensity product (Rossetto et al., 2011; Planès et al., 2014; Obermann et al., 2013b):

$$
1-\mathrm{CC}_{a,i}\;\approx\;\int_V K^{\mathrm{dec}}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r}_a,t_i)\,\delta\Sigma(\mathbf{x})\,d^3x,
\qquad
K^{\mathrm{dec}}\propto\frac{\int_0^t P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x},u)P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r},t-u)\,du}{P(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},t)} .
\tag{17}
$$

Use (16) for shear-velocity changes in the fault zone; use (17) when the change is a *scattering* change (e.g. fault-zone damage/healing) rather than a smooth velocity change.

## 8. Velocity change vs. source-location change

Your LFEs repeat, so the source can also *move* between realizations — and that is itself a target ("changes in the deep source location"). CWI separates the two cleanly because they leave different fingerprints in the coda (Snieder, 2006; Snieder & Vrijlandt, 2005).

- **Velocity change** → a *coherent* mean shift: $\langle\delta\tau\rangle/t=$ const $\neq 0$, so $\delta\tau$ **grows linearly with lapse time** $t$, with the same sign across the window. This is the "stretch."

- **Source displacement** $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ → a path-length change only on the source leg, $\delta\tau = -\boldsymbol{\delta}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}/\beta$, where $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ is the take-off direction. For an isotropic diffuse take-off distribution the **mean vanishes** but the **variance is nonzero and lapse-time-independent**:

$$
\langle\delta\tau\rangle=0,\qquad
\sigma_\tau^2=\langle\delta\tau^2\rangle=\frac{\delta^2}{3\,\beta^2}\quad(\text{single S mode}),
\tag{18}
$$

generalizing to an energy-weighted combination of $\alpha^{-2}$ and $\beta^{-2}$ when P is present (Snieder & Vrijlandt, 2005; Robinson et al., 2011; Singh et al., 2019).

**The discriminant:** fit each coda window for a stretch (coherent, $\propto t$) *and* a residual scatter (incoherent, flat in $t$).
- A nonzero, lapse-time-growing stretch that is **common-mode across the network** → a fault-zone velocity change (§6).
- A flat-in-$t$ decorrelation/variance with $\langle\delta\tau\rangle\approx0$ → a **source relocation** of magnitude $\delta=\sqrt{3}\,\beta\,\sigma_\tau$.

So the same coda gives you both the fault-zone $\delta\beta/\beta$ and the LFE migration distance, and they do not alias onto each other.

## 9. The inverse problem

### Step 7 — Discretize and assemble the sensitivity matrix

Partition the half-space into cells $V_j$, $j=1,\dots,M$, centered at $\mathbf{x}_j$. Stack observations over stations $a$ and lapse-time windows $i$ into a data vector $\mathbf{d}$ with entries $d_{ai}=(\delta\tau/\tau)_{a,i}$. The forward map (16) linearizes to

$$
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{m}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\qquad
G_{(ai),\,j}=-\,K(\mathbf{x}_j;\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r}_a,t_i)\,V_j,\qquad
m_j=\frac{\delta\beta}{\beta}(\mathbf{x}_j) .
\tag{19}
$$

Each row of $\mathbf{G}$ is one kernel (13) sampled on the grid; it has the deep lobe (shared by all rows with the same source) and a station-specific surface lobe.

### Step 8 — Regularized / Bayesian solution

The problem is ill-posed (smooth, overlapping kernels), so regularize. Tikhonov form:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{m}}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{m}}\;
\big\|\mathbf{G}\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{d}\big\|_{\mathbf{C}_d^{-1}}^2
+\lambda^2\big\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{m}\big\|^2 ,
\tag{20}
$$

with $\mathbf{L}$ a smoothing (Laplacian) or damping operator and $\mathbf{C}_d$ the data covariance. Equivalently, the Bayesian/Gaussian solution and its resolution:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{m}}=\big(\mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\mathbf{C}_d^{-1}\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{C}_m^{-1}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\mathbf{C}_d^{-1}\mathbf{d},
\qquad
\mathbf{R}=\big(\mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\mathbf{C}_d^{-1}\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{C}_m^{-1}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\mathbf{C}_d^{-1}\mathbf{G}.
\tag{21}
$$

Solve with LSQR/CGLS for large $M$. Inspect $\mathbf{R}$ (and checkerboard/point-spread tests) to see *where* the deep lobe + surface-lobe geometry actually resolves structure — the deep-lobe resolution is set by how many stations view the common source from different azimuths.

### Step 9 — Joint inversion for the deep source location

Append the source displacement to the model vector and linearize (18) about the current source estimate. Two practical routes:

1. **Decoupled (recommended first pass):** estimate the network-common stretch → velocity field via (19)–(21); separately estimate $\delta=\sqrt{3}\,\beta\,\sigma_\tau$ from the incoherent variance (§8). The orthogonality of the two fingerprints (coherent-$\propto t$ vs. incoherent-flat) keeps them from trading off.
2. **Fully joint:** extended model $\tilde{\mathbf{m}}=[\,\mathbf{m};\,\boldsymbol{\delta}\,]$ with an extended operator $[\mathbf{G}\,|\,\mathbf{H}]$, where $\mathbf{H}$ holds the linearized source-displacement partials $\partial(\delta\tau)/\partial\boldsymbol{\delta}=-\hat{\mathbf{n}}/\beta$ averaged over the take-off distribution per (station, lapse) pair. Solve (20)–(21) on $\tilde{\mathbf{m}}$.

### Step 10 — Network / experiment design implications

- **Multiple lapse-time windows per pair** → depth resolution (§6). Use them.
- **Azimuthal coverage of surface stations** → resolves the deep lobe (different surface lobes, shared deep lobe). A single station cannot separate "fault-zone change" from "change anywhere on my tube"; an azimuthal ring can.
- **Multiple LFE families** at different points along the slab interface → multiple source positions → along-strike and depth coverage of the fault zone, turning the monitoring into a sparse 4-D tomography of the source region.
- **$Q_i$ sets the usable lapse-time range**, not the kernel shape (§3, Step 5) — pick the latest windows for which the coda stays above noise to reach maximum diffusive (and hence deep) coverage.

---

## 10. Minimal computational recipe

1. Estimate $\beta$, $\ell^\*$ (hence $D=\beta\ell^\*/3$), and $Q_i$ for the Cascadia source-receiver volume from the LFE coda envelope decay.
2. For each (station $a$, lapse window $t_i$): evaluate the half-space kernel (13)–(14) on the 3-D grid by 1-D quadrature; for cells within $\ell^\*$ of a sensor, substitute the RTE/specific-intensity kernel (Margerin et al., 2016) or a Monte-Carlo RTE solution; switch to the ellipsoidal single-scattering kernel (15) for the earliest windows.
3. Measure, per pair and window, the stretch $(\delta\tau/\tau)_{a,i}$ and the residual decorrelation/variance.
4. Build $\mathbf{G}$ (19), invert (20)–(21) for the fault-zone $\delta\beta/\beta$ field; estimate the source displacement $\delta=\sqrt3\,\beta\,\sigma_\tau$ from the incoherent channel; optionally do the fully joint inversion (§9, route 2).
5. Validate against full-wavefield simulations with a planted thin-layer velocity perturbation at depth (the Obermann-style calibration, adapted to a buried source).
