### Deriving slip from GPS data PNW

Hundreds of GPS stations across the Pacific Northwest have been recording ground position every day since ~2007. Every few weeks, deep on the Cascadia subduction fault, a "slow slip event" happens — the fault slips a few centimetres over a couple of weeks instead of in seconds like an earthquake — and the GPS stations drift by a few millimetres in response. Gualandi's group in Cambridge runs an automated analysis **every day** that takes the _entire_ GPS record from 2007 up to that day and works out, from all those tiny surface motions, where and when the fault slipped over the whole 19-year period.

So the folder named 2026-02-24 is not "the data for February 24th." It's **the full 2007–2026 slip history, as computed on February** **24th**. Think of it like a nightly-rebuilt report: each day the analysis reruns on everything, so grabbing any one recent day's folder gives you the complete history. That's why downloading one date is enough, and why downloading many dates would only tell you how the _analysis itself_ wobbles as new data arrives — not more history.

  1. **Rebuild the slip movie.** The download stores slip in compressed form: about K ≈ a handful of fixed spatial _patterns_ on the fault (each pattern is "this region slips in this shape"), each with its own wiggling _time series_ saying how strongly that pattern is active on each day. Slip anywhere, any day = sum of the patterns × their amplitudes that day. The repo reads those files and reconstructs slip on a mesh of triangular fault patches through time.

  2. **Physics: slip → strain.** When a patch of fault slips, the surrounding rock deforms in a predictable way (standard elasticity theory — the Nikkhoo & Walter formulas, computed by the cutde library). The repo computes how much the crust is stretched/squeezed/sheared (**strain**) at a grid of points sitting 10 km above the fault surface. The trick that makes this fast: strain depends linearly on slip, so instead of computing the deformation for every one of ~7000 days, it computes it once per spatial pattern (K times) and reuses the same time series to animate it. Same answer, hundreds of times cheaper.

  3. **Strain → stress.** Using rock stiffness that varies with depth, strain is converted to **stress** — the actual pushing/pulling forces on the rock, including "Coulomb stress," which tells you whether the fault is being pushed toward or away from failure.

  4. **Smooth it to match the measurement you'll compare against.** The end goal is comparison with **dv/v** — tiny changes in seismic wave speed measured from ambient noise, which is sensitive to stress in the crust. Those dv/v measurements are inherently averaged over 31 days, so the repo runs its strain/stress through the exact same 31-day averaging. Apples to apples.

  5. **Output.** One netCDF file: the full strain and stress tensors plus summary quantities (dilatation, pressure, max shear, Coulomb stress...) on a longitude/latitude/time grid, plus diagnostic figures. The scientific question it feeds: _do slow slip events change_ stress in the crust enough, in the right places and times, to explain the dv/v signals people observe?
  
  6. One caveat baked into the data: the slip is measured _relative to the long-term tectonic trend_, so the steady background loading between slip events is absent by construction — you get the wiggles, not the ramp.
