# Pacific Northwest digital twin

A build plan for Claude Code, sized for a parallel-agent terminal workflow. Read `CLAUDE.md` first. This document tells each agent what to build, what contract it owns, and what done means.

## The thing we are building

One living, real-time digital twin of the Pacific Northwest as a single coupled system, scoped to Oregon, Washington, and British Columbia. It fuses the Salish Sea Cousin (the mechanistic model), WorldView (the 3D globe and its live human-pulse layers), and the five-plane control plane (sensing, observability, subsurface, decision) into one region on one clock. The Cousin explains, WorldView shows, the control plane fuses. The payoff is compound hazards, the couplings that define this region's risk, made spatial, live, and multi-physics.

## How to run this

You are the conductor. Open one terminal per plane and one for yourself. Each plane agent owns one directory tree and never edits another's. All agents synchronize through `packages/contracts`. When a shared type must change, change it in `packages/contracts` first, commit, then let the affected planes adapt.

Agent assignment:

- Agent A, Canvas, globe, and WorldView layers: `apps/web` globe surface, the human-pulse layers, and the 2D-to-3D bridge.
- Agent B, Observability: `workers/observability`, including the forecast path.
- Agent C, Sensing: `services/geocompute`, split across terminals. C1 solid-earth continuous and noise methods, C2 solid-earth event and network methods, C3 weather and hydromet, C4 multi-hazard normalizers.
- Agent D, Subsurface modeling: `services/geocompute` inversion and `apps/web` volume rendering.
- Agent E, Ontology, agents, and model extension: `packages/sim`, the ontology in `packages/contracts`, the Gemini agent in `workers/api`.
- Conductor (you): `packages/contracts`, the region bound, the clock and event bus, CI, integration, keeping the three modes green.

## Seismic and geodetic methods integration

All of these are geocompute modules under `services/geocompute/methods`. They differ only in cadence and output shape, and they all reduce to the shared contracts: continuous outputs become `Sample`, heavy outputs become `GeoProduct`, threshold crossings become `HazardEvent`, derived state nodes live in the ontology.

| Method | Cadence | Inputs | Emits | Detects |
|--------|---------|--------|-------|---------|
| dv/v | continuous, daily | ambient noise, pairs and autocorr | dv/v `Sample`, `dvvMatrix` | stress, fluids, thermal, hydrology, co-seismic and ETS-related change |
| H/V (HVSR) | continuous, rolling | 3-component noise, single station | f0 and A0 `Sample`, `hvsrCurve` | near-surface velocity, water table, damage |
| Receiver functions | per teleseism, accumulate | teleseismic events, broadband | conversion time and amplitude `Sample`, `rfStack` | crustal and slab structure, Vp/Vs, deep fluid change |
| Deep tremor | continuous | network waveforms | tremor rate `Sample`, episode `HazardEvent`, `tremorCatalog` | slow slip on the deep interface |
| GNSS | daily and high-rate | EarthScope NOTA positions | displacement and strain `Sample`, `gnssSeries` | crustal deformation, slow-slip surface reversal |

EarthScope is the source layer beneath this table, not a row in it. PNSN and ONC supply regional and offshore stations.

### The Cascadia payoff: an observable slow-slip composite

Deep tremor, a GNSS surface-displacement reversal, and a dv/v change are three views of one Episodic Tremor and Slip event on the deep Cascadia interface. The plan fuses them in one `CompositeMonitor` emitting `cascadia.slowSlipIndex`, and adds offshore seafloor geodesy (bottom pressure and GNSS-Acoustic) so the index sees the offshore updip portion of the megathrust where the tsunamigenic slip sits, not just the onshore downdip edge that land GNSS resolves. ETS in northern Cascadia recurs near a 14-month interval, so this index is genuinely trackable and is a real leading indicator of slow slip. Integrity boundary: slow slip is observable and quasi-periodic and loads the locked megathrust, but it is not a deterministic predictor of a great earthquake. The contract carries `isPrediction: false`, and the UI and agent describe it as ongoing slow slip, never a countdown.

## Weather and hydromet integration

This is the one domain that is allowed to predict, because numerical weather prediction is calibrated and skillful. The discipline is the contract split: observations and issued warnings are `HazardEvent` records, model output is a `Forecast`. They never mix.

| Product | Contract | Source | Role |
|---------|----------|--------|------|
| Station observations | `Sample` | NWS | current conditions |
| Issued warnings | `HazardEvent` (officialSource) | NWS alerts API | official, mirror only |
| Model forecasts | `Forecast` | HRRR, GFS, ECMWF | predictive, with stated skill |
| Radar | `GeoProduct` (radarSweep) | NEXRAD | precipitation field on the globe |
| Marine wind and waves | `Sample`, `Forecast` | WaveWatch III | vessel and ocean-mixing forcing |
| Storm surge and tides | `Sample`, `Forecast` | NOAA surge, Tides | coastal-flood forcing |
| Atmospheric river | metric and `Forecast` | model IVT | the region's signature winter hazard |
| Streamflow | `Sample` on `riverGauge` | USGS NWIS, Environment Canada | flood state |
| Snow water equivalent | `Sample` | SNOTEL | rain-on-snow setup |

## Offshore and marine geophysics integration

The Cascadia megathrust and the tsunami source are offshore, so a twin that stops sensing at the coastline is blind to where its defining hazard originates. This tier extends the solid earth and ocean state past the shelf. Like the others, everything reduces to the shared contracts.

| Product | Cadence | Source | Emits | Role |
|---------|---------|--------|-------|------|
| Seafloor pressure (vertical) | continuous | ONC, OOI bottom pressure | `Sample` | offshore vertical deformation, Axial inflation |
| GNSS-Acoustic (horizontal) | campaign | seafloor sites | `gnssAcoustic` | offshore strain into the slow-slip composite |
| DART tsunami | continuous | NDBC buoys | `Sample`, tsunami `HazardEvent` | the offshore tsunami sensor |
| Offshore OBS | continuous and archived | ONC, OOI, Cascadia Initiative via EarthScope | folds into FDSN | rebalances dv/v and tremor offshore |
| Axial inflation | continuous | OOI Cabled Array | `Sample`, labeled threshold readout | the one skillful volcanic forecast, scoped |
| Satellite ocean state | daily | PODAAC, Coral Reef Watch, Copernicus | `sstField`, MHW metric | marine heatwaves, upwelling |
| Shelf hypoxia and OA | continuous | OOI Oregon shelf, NANOOS | `Sample` | the Oregon dead zone into the calcifier nodes |
| Fishing effort and SAR | daily to weekly | Global Fishing Watch 4Wings | `fishingEffort`, `sarDetections` | offshore human pulse, fishing pressure |

Two honest limits. Seafloor geodesy is sparse and partly campaign-based, not a dense real-time network like land GNSS, so the offshore deformation signal is thinner and slower than the onshore one. And terrestrial AIS thins past the shelf, which is exactly why Global Fishing Watch matters here: its satellite AIS and SAR detections see the vessels, including the dark ones, that AISStream misses offshore.

The Axial nuance, restated because it is the one place the prediction line bends. Axial Seamount inflates to a fairly repeatable threshold before it erupts, and that has produced genuinely skillful eruption windows. The plan treats this as an explicitly labeled, evidence-based threshold readout with a window, never a precise date, never generalized to other volcanoes or to earthquakes. It is a `Sample` and a labeled state, not a `Forecast`.

## Compound hazards

This is why the twin exists rather than a set of separate dashboards. The region's signature risks are couplings, and the Cousin already models couplings, so the work is to make them spatial, live, and multi-physics by wiring atmosphere and hydrology into the existing graph as edges:

- Atmospheric river onto a burn scar drives the debris-flow and sediment term the Cousin already has.
- Rain on snow, the SNOTEL layer against an AR, drives a flood through the river-gauge nodes.
- Storm surge stacked on a king tide on rising sea level drives coastal flooding.
- Vessel noise from the AIS layer against orca foraging efficiency, an edge the Cousin already encodes.
- Fishing effort from Global Fishing Watch against salmon and groundfish, a fishing-mortality edge onto the prey base the resident orca depend on.
- Cascadia under all of it, where a great-earthquake scenario cascades into tsunami, liquefaction, and port and population exposure.

Each coupling is a `CausalEdge` with a mechanism and, where possible, a citation. Uncalibrated couplings are marked `calibrated: false`.

## Transboundary handling

Roughly half the data is United States and half Canadian, under two regimes, and Coast Salish sovereignty crosses the border. Tag every source and entity with jurisdiction. Reconcile units, datums, and cadences at ingest, not in the UI. Treaty and Usual and Accustomed data and sensitive ecological locations are flagged and handled with consent. The border is a data-engineering and ethics constraint, not a visual one. The twin renders one continuous region.

Pick one to wire first. Both touch every plane.

- Cascadia slow-slip and tremor: shows off the seismic depth. Tremor plus GNSS plus dv/v fuse into the slow-slip index, render on the globe, and the agent explains the ongoing ETS without forecasting a quake.

## Risks and honest caveats

- The two-contract prediction line is the central integrity risk. Weather may forecast, the solid earth may not, and the contracts keep them apart. A polished command center invites over-reading, so this is load-bearing.
- This is the Pacific Northwest only. It is not a global system, and scope creep beyond OR, WA, BC is a v1 failure.
- It does not replace official warnings. It mirrors and contextualizes ShakeAlert, the NWS, and the tsunami centers, with `officialSource` set, and never presents itself as the authority.
- Transboundary data is two regimes and two sets of units and licenses. Reconcile at ingest. Sovereignty is consent, not a flag toggled after the fact.
- Cost: Google Photorealistic 3D Tiles is an Enterprise SKU. Cache aggressively, consider NASA GIBS or self-hosted tiles for heavy public traffic.
- The geocompute service is the one non-edge piece. Keep it small so it runs on modest hardware.
- Offshore data is thinner than onshore. Seafloor geodesy is sparse and partly campaign-based, not a dense real-time network, so offshore deformation is slower and less certain. Terrestrial AIS thins past the shelf, which is why Global Fishing Watch satellite AIS and SAR are the offshore vessel picture. State these limits in the UI rather than implying uniform coverage.
- Axial is the only solid-earth forecast the plan allows, scoped and labeled. Do not let it soften the no-forecast rule for earthquakes or other volcanoes.
- The line between an impressive demo and a real instrument is calibrated baselines, real inversions, and stated forecast skill, not a prettier overlay.

**The Salish Sea Digital Cousin: An Open-Source Prototype of Coupled Socio-Ecological Modeling for an Urban Sea System**

_M. Ryan Delaney, Haas School of Business, University of California, Berkeley_

# A.1  Introduction

The Salish Sea Digital Cousin is a browser-based simulation of the Salish Sea region. Twenty-four compute modules organized into eight coupling domains share a quarterly time step over a fifty-year horizon, draw on 76 live public-sector data endpoints, and surface through four audience-specific user interfaces. It runs in any modern web browser, requires no installation, and is freely accessible.

This Appendix describes that artifact and the claim its existence supports: that the integration challenge the main paper develops conceptually is now tractable at single-developer scale. Five years ago it was not. The Cousin is one empirical data point for that change.

The close of Section 4.2 introduces the artifact as an “initial prototype of [the Urban Sea System Digital Twin] architecture, developed as an independent open-source project and deliberately designated a ‘Digital Cousin’ rather than a Digital Twin to reflect its status as a teaching and scenario tool rather than a physics-accurate replica.” Digital Twin literature converges on three features: a virtual computational representation, bi-directional data flow with the physical system, and decision support through scenario exploration. The Cousin satisfies the first and third constraint. Its data flow is one-way: observations feed in, but the simulation does not write back to any physical system. It is also not built to operate persistently over decadal timescales. Against the three-aspect extension this paper proposes for Urban Sea System Digital Twins specifically (multi-module by necessity, governance-facing by design, persistent rather than project-bounded), the Cousin satisfies the first two and does not satisfy the third. It is a teaching and scenario tool that demonstrates what a Twin’s coupling architecture might look like at operational scale, not a Twin itself.

The empirical claim is bounded but specific. A combined architecture covering ocean physics, marine ecology, port economics, contaminant transport, climate forcing, infrastructure resilience, and tribal governance was assembled, united through explicit shared state, visible through four audience-specific interfaces, and validated against fifteen years of historical observations. The Cousin does not approach the operational Salish Sea Model (PNNL), LiveOcean (UW), or SalishSeaCast, and is not intended to. Its contribution lies in showing that integration architecture itself is now within reach.

The Cousin was developed in the San Francisco Bay Area, where AI-assisted software development has become a dominant working tool for technical projects. The artifact reflects what is now achievable by a single developer with access to current AI tools and a focused domain target, working on a timescale of weeks rather than years. This regional and methodological context is part of the empirical claim. Coupled socio-ecological modeling of this kind was, until recently, the domain of multi-year team efforts at well-resourced institutions. That is changing rapidly, and the Cousin is one instance of what becomes possible at the leading edge of that change.

This Appendix is organized into five additional sections. Section A.2 describes the architecture, what it demonstrates, and supporting module and coupling taxonomy. Section A.3 walks through the Cascadia M9 earthquake recovery scenario as a worked example, exercising the cascading-failure dynamics that conventional sector-by-sector planning treats incompletely (§6). Section A.4 provides an accounting of validation, parameterization, structural limitations, and known gaps. Section A.5 closes with reflection on the Cousin’s development, where it sits in the lineage of related simulation work, and how it might inform Digital Twin development that the main paper proposes. Section A.6 contains a competing-interests disclaimer.

The Cousin is independent of any institutional Digital Twin development effort. Its purpose is to make a complex coupled system inspectable and teachable: to a knowledgeable audience of practitioners, scientists, and policy actors who already understand the Salish Sea, and through them to the broader community that may engage with the Workshop and the Digital Twin development effort the main paper sets out.

# A.2  Architecture

The architecture demonstrates three integration patterns the main paper argues are load-bearing for Urban Sea System Digital Twin development. First, combined modules with shared state and orchestrated convergence. Second, one-way data ingestion at scale across public-sector sources. Third, audience-specific interfaces consuming a single computational substrate rather than parallel platform implementations. Each is described below with the supporting module and coupling detail.

The Cousin runs entirely client-side in JavaScript with no server-side computation. The choice eliminates infrastructure dependencies, makes the artifact freely accessible to anyone with a web browser, and removes the institutional commitments that operating a server-backed system over time would require. The trade-off is computational scale: the Cousin operates at quarterly time steps over a fifty-year horizon (200 simulation steps total). That resolution is appropriate to a teaching and scenario tool but would be insufficient for operational forecasting.

## A.2.1  Combined modules with explicit coupling

The main paper’s discussion of Figure 4 states: “the critical intellectual and engineering challenge is not the development of individual modules, most of which already exist in mature form, but the construction of the couplings among them — the exchanges of state, flux, and boundary conditions that allow system-level feedbacks and emergent behavior to be represented.” The Cousin’s design follows this principle directly. Individual modules are simpler than their domain-specific operational counterparts. What the Cousin demonstrates is that the couplings among them can be made explicit and computationally tractable at the scale of a full system.

The simulation engine consists of twenty-four compute modules organized into eight coupling domains: (1) ocean physics, including tidal exchange, stratification, and dissolved-oxygen dynamics across marine basins; (2) marine ecology, including 42 species from primary producers to orcas with explicit predator-prey and habitat coupling; (3) carbonate chemistry, implementing CO2SYS for full speciation across the dissolved-inorganic-carbon system; (4) climate forcing, including atmospheric and oceanic boundary conditions and propagating them through downstream modules; (5) port economics and supply chain, covering Vancouver, Seattle, Tacoma, and Olympia terminal operations and regional employment; (6) infrastructure resilience, including I-5 corridor, ferry network, and grid reliability; (7) tribal governance, highlighting the 34 sovereign Indigenous nations whose treaty rights and co-management responsibilities span the basin; and (8) hazards, including the Cascadia M9 earthquake scenario, atmospheric-river risks, and marine heatwave dynamics.

Figure 5 of the main paper identifies sixteen SubMod domains that an operational Urban Sea Digital Twin would need to represent. The Cousin’s twenty-four compute modules constitute one particular implementation that covers most of those domains and adds several that emerged from the prototype’s scope: an individual-based Southern Resident orca model with 73 named whales drawn from the Center for Whale Research 2024 census; a Cascadia M9 earthquake scenario module; speciated contaminant transport with biomagnification through the food web. The mismatch between sixteen and twenty-four reflects different decompositions for different purposes. Figure 5 enumerates the domains that must be represented; the Cousin enumerates the compute modules that implement a representation.

Three coupling examples illustrate how state flows across the combined module set. In the first, what the project calls the Cascadia hero cascade, Federal Reserve housing-and-rates data drives a regional development-pressure index, which drives shoreline armoring, which drives eelgrass habitat loss, which drives Pacific herring forage availability, which drives Chinook salmon condition, which drives Southern Resident orca prey base. Each link is engine-backed, with explicit state-contract exchanges between modules. The carbonate chemistry chain follows a similar pattern: atmospheric CO₂ forcing drives ocean uptake, then speciated DIC and pH and aragonite-saturation calculations, then shellfish larvae viability and Bigg’s orca contaminant burden. The M9 disaster coupling propagates an instantaneous event through infrastructure damage, port-throughput collapse, supply-chain disruption, and multi-year recovery dynamics across marine, economic, infrastructure, and community-health domains. Section A.3 walks the M9 scenario in detail.

The simulation step orchestrates these couplings via a three-iteration convergence on the watershed-port-urban coupling, where feedback loops are tightest. The broader module set updates within the converged context. The twenty-four compute modules consume from a shared state object and emit updates that the orchestrator merges into the next quarterly step. Combined modules, explicit shared state, orchestrated convergence as described in the main paper where it argues that the Twin architecture is “inherently federated rather than monolithic.”

## A.2.2  One-way data ingestion at scale

The Cousin connects to 76 live data endpoints across federal, state, provincial, tribal, and academic sources. These include NOAA tide and meteorological stations, Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) economic indicators, Washington State Department of Transportation traffic and ferry feeds, AirNow air quality monitoring, USGS stream gauges, and Ocean Networks Canada cabled-array streams. The data ingestion layer is one-directional. Real-time observations feed the simulation’s initial conditions and certain ongoing forcing terms; the simulation does not write back to any physical system. This is one of the principal ways the Cousin differs from the bi-directional data flow that defines a Digital Twin per the main paper’s §4 criteria. An operational Twin would close this loop, with simulation outputs informing the operation or configuration of the physical system.

Of the 76 endpoints, approximately 31 are currently live and continuously polled. The remainder are wired but operate against cached or scheduled-pull data sources rather than streaming feeds. The asymmetry is operational, not architectural: the wiring is in place, and full streaming would require either lightweight server infrastructure or unification with existing observing-system APIs that operate behind authentication. Both are feasible extensions, neither necessary for the Cousin’s purpose as a teaching and scenario tool. The unification challenge across the heterogeneous public-sector observational landscape (different agencies, different access regimes, different update cadences, different schemas) is computationally tractable at the scale at which Urban Sea System Digital Twins would need to operate. The Cousin’s data layer is evidence of that.

## A.2.3  Audience-specific interfaces from a common substrate

The Cousin presents the simulation through four primary user-facing experiences, with a fifth governance-lab module in development, each tuned to a distinct audience. The Explorer interface is built for general public and educational users, presenting the simulation as a narrative-scaffolded “what if” experience with scenario cards, story beats, and a map. The Policy interface is built for policymakers and regulators, presenting the same simulation as a decision-support environment with policy bundles, parameter sliders, and expected-outcome projections. The Research interface is built for scientists and analysts, presenting the simulation as a dense information environment with multi-panel gauges, time-series charts, and direct access to model state. The Briefing interface is built for executive and funder audiences, presenting an executive summary with risk taxonomy, KPI grid, and policy recommendations.

Each user experience runs on the same simulation engine, data feeds, and module state, however each visual interfeace is tuned to what each audience needs to see. A user who activates a scenario from the Explorer interface (for example, “What if the big earthquake hits?”) sees that scenario’s state propagate to the Policy Dashboard’s recovery panels, the Research Dashboard’s sub-basin grid, and the Briefing’s executive summary, each rendered in its surface’s own register. Scenario activation can also happen from Policy’s Scenarios listing. Engine state is the single observable shared across all surfaces. An in-app mode-switcher allows direct navigation among the four interfaces, with simulation state and active scenario preserved across transitions. A user can activate a scenario in Explorer, navigate to Policy to see policy implications, navigate to Research to inspect the underlying engine state, and navigate to Briefing for the executive summary, without losing simulation context at any step.

The main paper observes that “real value emerges when the system allows different users — scientists, planners, emergency managers, port authorities, regulators, communities — to explore conditions, test assumptions, and compare scenarios.” The Cousin demonstrates that a single underlying model can support multiple audience-specific interfaces from the same computational substrate. Audience differentiation is otherwise addressed through parallel platforms; this is a pattern worth carrying forward into Digital Twin development.

# A.3  Worked Example: Cascadia M9 Recovery Scenario

Section 6 of the main paper develops the Cascadia M9 scenario as the strongest practical argument for the Digital Twin framework. The initial seismic shock cannot be predicted. The secondary cascading failures can be explored with greater rigor than is commonly done. The recovery dynamics extend across years rather than weeks. Seasonal context matters: a major rupture during atmospheric-river season, with saturated soils, elevated river systems, vulnerable transportation corridors, and grid stress already present, produces compound failure modes that conventional sector-by-sector planning treats incompletely. This section walks through what the Cousin simulates when the M9 scenario is activated.

The scenario activates an instantaneous event at simulation time t₀ and propagates its consequences across the coupled module set over subsequent quarterly steps. Shock distribution is sub-basin-resolved across eighteen basins in the Salish Sea. Peak Ground Acceleration values are drawn from the USGS M9 ShakeMap scenario (Atkinson and Adams 2003). Liquefaction susceptibility is drawn from the Washington Department of Natural Resources Liquefaction Susceptibility Map and the USGS Seattle Urban Hazard Map. Tsunami arrival times and wave heights are drawn from PMEL tsunami modeling (Walsh et al. 2000) for the Cascadia Subduction Zone shelf-rupture tsunami, augmented by a Fraser Delta submarine landslide tsunami envelope (Rabinovich et al. 2003). The magnitude range of [8.7, 9.2] is calibrated to Satake et al. (2003), using the moment-magnitude derivation from Japanese tsunami-height records of the January 1700 Cascadia event. Macro-economic shock magnitudes and casualty range estimates are calibrated against FEMA Cascadia Rising exercise scenarios (2016, 2022) and CREW reports. Combined sub-basin damage compounds shaking, liquefaction, and tsunami damage multiplicatively, modulated by a preparedness factor that maps regional infrastructure investment to a damage-reduction modifier.

Civilian recovery dynamics unfold over a five-phase progression that the Cousin tracks across simulation quarters. In phase one (immediate response, weeks 0–4), port throughput is near zero, the ferry network is offline, the I-5 corridor is severed at multiple points, and emergency supply distribution is constrained by transportation collapse. In phase two (stabilization, months 1–6), partial port reopening occurs through alternative terminals, the ferry network restores on routes whose terminal damage permits it, and I-5 detour reconstruction begins. In phase three (reconstruction, months 6–18), infrastructure investment ramps up, supply chains reroute through Canadian and southern alternatives, and the first significant ecological signals emerge as marine sediment disturbance and coastal contamination work through the food web. In phase four (ecological response, years 1–3), contaminant releases, habitat damage, and disrupted fisheries propagate through marine ecology modules, with explicit visibility into Southern Resident orca prey-base impacts via the salmon-herring-eelgrass coupling chain. In phase five (structural transformation, years 3–5 and beyond), the long-tail dynamics become visible: regional economic restructuring as supply chains permanently shift, demographic redistribution, and the slow trajectory of marine ecosystem recovery.

Recovery trajectories are computed as exponential decay curves with category-specific time constants: infrastructure takes approximately four years to achieve 63% recovery, environmental ten years, economic three years, and population two years. These constants are framed against Tohoku-oki and Cascadia Rising but are not directly fitted to post-event recovery data. The recovery counter advances through 80 quarterly steps (twenty years), at which point the M9 scenario state releases cleanly and baseline dynamics resume. The twenty-year horizon is a structural choice rather than a literature-grounded recovery boundary; Section A.4 discusses this and other parameterization choices in more detail.

The Cousin makes these phase transitions inspectable across all four user-facing interfaces. The Explorer surface presents narrative-scaffolded story beats and a trajectory readout with two-year-projection summaries as the user steps the simulation forward. The Policy Dashboard shows a Cascadia M9 recovery panel with infrastructure, economic, population, and environmental recovery quartiles plus a port-capacity segment. Dashboard-body indicators reflect M9-driven engine state, and a dedicated M9 Detail tab appears in the Policy navigation when the M9 scenario is active. The Research Dashboard shows an orientation header indicating M9 is active, a dedicated Cascadia M9 status panel showing recovery quartiles and port capacity, and an eighteen-sub-basin grid reflecting the cascading damage pattern across marine, ecosystem, infrastructure, and economic indicators. The M9 Detail tab provides a six-panel deep-dive view: sub-basin damage tables, terminal-by-terminal port impact, environmental layer detail with tribal sites flagged, macroeconomic trajectory and labor impact, recovery-curve trajectories with current-quarter and twenty-year terminus markers, and casualty and public-health detail. The Briefing interface includes a “Cascadia M9 recovery year N” sentence to its executive summary, presents a visible Cascadia M9 panel between the executive summary and key indicators, and includes a CASCADIA M9 section in the clipboard output for external sharing. Activation can occur from the Explorer scenario card or from the Policy Scenarios listing. In both cases the engine state propagates identically through all four surfaces.

The Cousin also allows the user to compare scenario trajectories: a baseline with no M9 event; an M9 event at the simulation’s default seasonal timing; and, if the user chooses, variant scenarios that vary the preparedness posture (the seismic_preparedness scenario) or layer M9 onto a 2040 climate context (the big_one_plus_climate scenario). The comparative-trajectory pattern is what the main paper describes when it argues that a Twin can move planning “from generalized awareness toward more specific, community-relevant resilience strategies.”

Several aspects of the M9 scenario are deliberately out of scope. Tsunami arrivals and wave-height envelopes are represented at the sub-basin level, but parcel-level tsunami inundation modeling, which would require running specialist tools such as NOAA SIFT or ComMIT against high-resolution coastal bathymetry, is not. Building-specific structural damage is not represented; building codes, structural engineering models, and parcel-level damage assessment are domains of mature specialist modeling that an operational Twin would federate rather than reproduce. Aftershock sequences, time-varying tsunami inundation, and rebuilding endogeneity (preparedness investments cannot rise during recovery in the current implementation) are similarly deliberate simplifications. Defense and military infrastructure implications are not represented; the Cousin focuses on civilian recovery dynamics as appropriate to the main paper’s framingTthe Cousin shows the system-level propagation of damage through coupled domains, which is precisely what the main paper argues conventional planning treats incompletely.

# A.4  Honest Accounting

This Appendix endeavors to report what the Cousin can do, what it cannot, and which limitations are inherent versus addressable. Three subsections follow: what has been validated, what is bounded by structural choices, and what is deferred.

## A.4.1  What has been validated

The Cousin’s primary validation metric is hindcast skill against the 2010–2025 historical period. The simulation is initialized at the start of 2010, run forward over 60 quarterly steps, and its trajectory compared against observational data for the same period across ten validation variables. Core hindcast skill (Pearson R, computed across the ten-variable set) is currently 0.71. This represents substantive improvement from an earlier baseline of 0.68, achieved through carbonate chemistry refinements (CO2SYS implementation) and sediment phase-coupling work.

Per-variable skill varies considerably. Port-TEU throughput tracks at R ≈ 0.999, reflecting that the Cousin’s economic and supply-chain modules are well-validated against published port statistics. Puget Sound Chinook salmon tracks at R ≈ 0.88. Sea-surface temperature tracks at R ≈ 0.76, comparable to Hood Canal dissolved oxygen at R ≈ 0.76. Puget Sound pink salmon tracks at R ≈ 0.74. The Southern Resident orca population tracks at R ≈ 0.70, a skill level that on inspection contains a structural error component rather than purely random noise (discussed below in A.4.2). Fraser River sockeye tracks at R ≈ 0.70. Other variables fall in the 0.5–0.7 range.

The Cousin’s parameterization is heterogeneous. When possible, inputs are grounded in published primary sources and are directly citable. Others are hand-tuned for plausible behavior consistent with the broader literature but are not derived from a specific paper. Parameters with mechanistic grounding in primary sources include: the Cascadia magnitude range [8.7, 9.2] from Satake, Wang, and Atwater (2003); Peak Ground Acceleration distribution from the USGS M9 ShakeMap scenario (Atkinson and Adams 2003); liquefaction susceptibility from the Washington Department of Natural Resources and USGS Seattle Urban Hazard maps; tsunami arrival times and wave heights from PMEL tsunami modeling (Walsh et al. 2000), augmented by a Fraser Delta submarine landslide tsunami envelope (Rabinovich et al. 2003); casualty range estimates and economic-loss bands calibrated against FEMA Cascadia Rising exercise scenarios (2016, 2022) and CREW reports; spawning-beach recovery time constants from Penttila (2007); and aquaculture and farmed-salmon escape parameters from Naylor et al. (2005). Parameters that are hand-tuned for plausible behavior, framed against literature context but not citation-grounded, include: the four recovery time constants used to compute exponential recovery curves (infrastructure τ = 16 quarters, environmental τ = 40 quarters, economic τ = 12 quarters, population τ = 8 quarters); the shaking-damage nonlinearity exponent (1.5 in the PGA-to-damage mapping); the 15-meter tsunami-to-90%-damage calibration; the per-terminal vulnerability fractions for the 26 named port and refinery terminals; the per-system infrastructure damage weights; and the contamination transport graph between sub-basins.

## A.4.2  What is bounded by structural choices

The orca skill of R ≈ 0.70 contains a structural error component identified through validation investigation. The error pre-dates the carbonate chemistry refinements and reflects limitations in the individual-based orca model’s coupling to prey availability rather than randomness. The structural error is documented in the project’s known-issues record and represents a bounded, investigable limitation rather than a general failure of the orca module.

The ocean acidification (OA) chain through carbonate chemistry into shellfish viability and orca contaminant burden is currently latent. The chain is computed but its outputs are not yet fully surfaced to user-facing interfaces. This is an implementation gap, not a modeling gap; the underlying physics is in place. Surfacing the chain end-to-end is a near-term development priority.

The Cousin’s pH validation is bounded by an observational ceiling. High-resolution pH observational data for the Salish Sea is comparatively limited relative to the temperature and dissolved-oxygen observational records, which means the Cousin’s pH skill cannot be validated to the same precision as other variables. The operational Salish Sea Model and SalishSeaCast navigate the same constraint. It reflects a regional observational gap that several investigators are working to address.

There are several limitations specific to the M9 scenario implementation. The recovery window terminates structurally at twenty years post-event, after which the M9 scenario state releases and baseline dynamics resume; this twenty-year horizon is a structural choice rather than a literature-grounded recovery boundary. The marine contamination and sediment-mobilization pathway in the M9 module is currently dormant under the orchestrator’s wiring (the function is invoked with a null marine-state argument); marine M9 effects flow through an independent, simpler shock pathway that models co-seismic subsidence and liquefaction intensity at coarser sub-basin grouping. The preparedness modifier in the M9 dynamics is a parameter: only the I-5 maintenance investment slider feeds it. The other infrastructure investment sliders affect recovery initial conditions but do not modulate the shock magnitude. Casualty estimates are presented as prose-only output and do not feed back into engine state; population displacement does not propagate to the urban module. Fraser Delta submarine landslide collapse is modeled as binary and certain on every M9 event, with no probabilistic slope-stability model.

Several aspects are bounded by deliberate scope rather than implementation gap. The Cousin does not attempt to deliver physics-accurate forecasting. It does not attempt to substitute for operational modeling efforts that have decades of investment and domain-specific validation behind them; Section A.4.3 below names the appropriate federated dependencies. It does not attempt to represent the institutional, governance, and stakeholder coordination that the main paper argues constitutes the binding constraint on Twin development; that work remains genuinely human and no software artifact can replace it. And it does not attempt persistence: the Cousin is a teaching and scenario tool that may be developed further, but it is not built to operate over the decadal timescales an operational Twin requires. Each of these is a positive scoping choice, not an unaddressed limitation.

## A.4.3  What is deferred

Several gaps in the Cousin remain unaddressed. Outer-coast upwelling boundary forcing is currently parameterized rather than coupled to a regional ocean model; an operational Twin would couple to LiveOcean or a comparable source for this. Sediment diagenesis is implemented at advisory level: the mechanistic sediment-oxygen-demand module is dimensionally honest but provides advisory rather than authoritative deep-DO output. Uncertainty quantification is not yet implemented; the Cousin produces deterministic single-trajectory outputs rather than ensemble distributions, which limits its utility for risk assessment and is a substantial development priority. Satellite-data integration is not yet implemented; ocean-color, sea-surface-temperature, and altimetry products would substantially strengthen the Cousin’s representation of the marine layer. Validation against post-event recovery data from analog M9 events, particularly Tohoku-oki 2011, would shift the M9 recovery time-constant parameterization from prose-justified to literature-fitted; this is a tractable extension that has not yet been undertaken.

These gaps are documented in the project’s comparative analysis against PNNL Salish Sea Model, UW LiveOcean, CSIRO Atlantis, and EU Digital Twin Ocean. None blocking the Cousin’s purpose as a teaching and scenario tool, and all are on the path to implementation as the project continues to develop.

The Cousin is not a competitor to PNNL’s Salish Sea Model (Khangaonkar et al. 2012, 2018), UW’s LiveOcean (MacCready et al. 2009, 2010, 2025), or SalishSeaCast. Those operational models are the appropriate federated dependencies that an operational Salish Digital Twin would coordinate among, each providing physics-accurate domain modeling at spatial and temporal resolutions directly relevant to management. The Cousin operates at quarterly timesteps with deliberately simplified domain modules; its purpose is to demonstrate the integration architecture, not to provide operational forecasts. Comparison against the operational models on per-variable skill is not the right metric. The right metric is whether the Cousin demonstrates that the integration architecture is computationally tractable, governance-facing, and audience-tunable.

Looking outward to the international Digital Twin Ocean (DTO) effort, the Cousin sits in a comparable role to the EU Digital Twin Ocean prototypes and the CSIRO Atlantis end-to-end ecosystem model. It is a coupled socio-ecological simulation tool that demonstrates integration patterns rather than serving as an operational forecasting system. The architectural patterns it demonstrates (federated modules, explicit shared state, and audience-specific interfaces from a common substrate) are directly portable to operational Twin development. The Cousin is a teaching tool that may inform the architectural choices of operational efforts.

# A.5  What the Cousin Demonstrates and Where It Sits

 The integration architecture the main paper develops conceptually is now computationally tractable at single-developer scale. Five years ago, the cost, complexity, and team-size required for the kind of multi-module coupled simulation the Cousin demonstrates would have placed it firmly in major-institutional-commitment territory. Section 10 of the main paper makes the abstract claim that AI-enabled productivity has changed what is possible and at what cost. The Cousin is a concrete data point in support of that claim.

The scenario-exploration approach extends an intellectual lineage exemplified by the Chesapeake Bay Game (Learmonth et al. 2011), in which participants take stakeholder roles and explore the consequences of decisions within a simulated watershed-management context. The Bay Game demonstrated, at the resolution its era permitted, what immersive participatory simulation could do for systems-thinking education and for stakeholder engagement with complex environmental decisions. The scale of what is possible has shifted since then. Modern computing, real-time data ingestion, AI-assisted development workflows, and interactive visualization make it feasible to ground simulation environments in the actual coupled behavior of specific Urban Sea Systems rather than stylized abstraction. The Cousin extends the Bay Game’s approach to a higher-resolution, multi-audience, system-of-systems target. The gap between simulation-as-concept and simulation-as-deployed-reality has not changed. The governance, institutional, and adoption challenges the Bay Game navigated remain the same challenges any Urban Sea System Digital Twin will encounter. The Workshop the main paper proposes is the venue where those challenges will be addressed; the Cousin is a working substrate to inform those conversations, not a substitute for them.

Several development directions are anticipated as the project continues toward the Workshop and Twin development the main paper proposes. Integration with reinsurance and parametric-insurance risk-modeling frameworks is a near-term priority. Section 7 of the main paper identifies insurance and reinsurance firms as one of the principal private-sector participants whose engagement with Urban Sea System Digital Twin infrastructure has structural rather than incidental motivation; the compound-risk assessment described in Section 6 is precisely the analytical surface where reinsurance-grade risk modeling intersects with integrated system simulation. The Cousin’s existing M9 worked example surfaces the cascading-failure dynamics that reinsurance modeling would integrate with. What remains is to wire those dynamics into return-period exposure and capital-adequacy frameworks that industry actors can engage with directly. This work is appropriately scoped for post-paper development with industry consultation rather than asserted as current capability. Other anticipated extensions include uncertainty quantification (ensemble-trajectory rather than single-trajectory outputs), satellite-data integration for the marine layer, federation with operational forecasting models for boundary forcing, and validation against analog M9 recovery data.

In its current form, the Cousin offers the Workshop and Twin development the main paper proposes a working substrate. Architectural choices can be evaluated against it. Audience-specific interface patterns can be inspected. Cascading-failure dynamics that conventional planning treats incompletely can be made visible. The integration challenge can be engaged concretely rather than abstractly. The Cousin’s open-source status, independent of any institutional Digital Twin development, means it can be inspected, modified, or extended by anyone who finds its patterns useful. It is, in the literal sense, a cousin to the Twin work this paper advocates: related and supportive, but not the same thing.
