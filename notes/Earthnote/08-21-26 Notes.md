
The goal: turn thousands of individual dv/v time series (one per LFE family per station) into a single daily movie of how seismic velocity
  changes on the deep plate interface under Cascadia.

  The problem with the raw measurements: each family's dv/v series mixes three things together:
  1. The thing we want — velocity change down on the fault where the LFE waves travel
  2. Stuff local to the station — shallow ground effects, weather, seasonal wetting/drying, which affects every family recorded at that station the
     same way
  3. An arbitrary constant baseline — dv/v is always measured relative to a reference stack, so each family's series has a meaningless offset;
     worse, that offset jumps when the instrument or its response changes

  What the inversion does: for every day, it takes all the measurements from all families at all stations and splits them into exactly those three
  parts, solved jointly:
  - a fault map m(x,t) — velocity change in each ~8 km cell of the interface, per day
  - a site term per station per day — soaks up everything station-local (this replaces the old seasonal-correction step: since it's estimated fresh
    each day, it absorbs seasonality automatically)
  - a constant offset per family per instrument era — soaks up the arbitrary baselines and the instrument-change jumps (this is what finally fixed
    the WISH-2011 / LCCR-2013 leak problem)

  Each measurement "votes" on the fault cells its coda waves actually sampled (its kernel), so a cell's value comes from the agreement among many
  crossing family–station paths — that's what lets us separate "the fault changed" from "one station is being weird."

  Why this design matters vs. the old annual version: the old approach subtracted each series' own mean first, which quietly erased any real slow
  multi-year change in the fault. The new setup keeps offsets as explicit unknowns tied to one common reference window (2019–2024), so slow
  differential changes in the fault survive — that's the whole point, since long-term deep strain is the target now.

  How it's solved: alternate between "given the offsets, solve each day's map" and "given the maps, update the offsets," repeating until nothing
  moves. We first proved on a small subset that this shortcut gives the same answer as solving everything at once (this morning's validator), then
  ran the full thing: 30 million measurements, 6,354 days, 188 stations.

  Honest caveats: any single day's map is noise-dominated (uncertainty ~0.7% per cell vs. signals of ~0.04%), so the movie is for watching coverage
  and slow structure — real claims will come from averaging over time, with significance tests still to be built. And absolute level is
  unknowable: everything is relative to the 2019–2024 mean.
