Each 60-day bin gives a table of dv/v: families as rows, stations as columns. The separation splits that table into three parts.

**Example:** 2 families (A, B) and 3 stations, one time bin, dv/v in %:

||Station 1|Station 2|Station 3|**Row average**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|Family A|−0.05|−0.25|+0.15|**−0.05**|
|Family B|−0.15|−0.35|+0.05|**−0.15**|
|**Column average**|−0.10|−0.30|+0.10|**overall −0.10**|

1. **Common = the overall average:** −0.10%. Every path shares it.
2. **Source (deep) term = how far a row sits from the overall average:** A = −0.05 − (−0.10) = **+0.05**, B = **−0.05**. Family A is faster than average at every station, so the difference must sit on A's side of the paths, near A's source.
3. **Receiver (shallow) term = how far a column sits from the overall average:** station 1 = **0**, station 2 = **−0.20**, station 3 = **+0.20**. Station 2 is slower for every family, so the difference must sit under station 2.
4. **Check:** every cell = common + row term + column term. For example, A at station 2 = −0.10 + 0.05 − 0.20 = −0.25 ✓. Anything left over is noise.

**The real version differs in a few ways:**

- Not every family is recorded at every station, and measurements are noisy, so it's a least-squares fit rather than simple averages.
- It's done for all 115 bins at once, with a little smoothing in time.
- Each family–station series is measured against its own reference, so a constant offset per series is removed first.

**The key consequence:** the source and receiver terms average to zero by construction. Anything that happens to all families and all stations together can only show up in the common term. So the common term can't say whether it's deep or shallow; that needs outside evidence such as the noise dv/v.

Here's the reasoning in simple steps.

**1. A repeating source gives a repeatable clock.** Each LFE family fires the same way thousands of times, so its waves look the same every time. Stacking 60 days of events and asking "did S arrive earlier or later than usual?" measures the arrival to a millisecond or two.

**2. The event times themselves can wobble.** If the catalogue's time for a batch of events is off by 2 ms, every station sees S "2 ms late." So one station on its own can't tell a change in the rock from a timing error.

**3. Comparing stations cancels that.** A timing error moves all stations equally. Subtracting one station's shift from another's removes it, and what's left is a real difference between their paths.

**4. Boreholes against surface is the key pair.** The PB borehole sensors sit a few hundred metres down. A wave from a family 30 km deep travels almost the same path to a borehole and to a nearby surface station, except that the surface wave also crosses the top few hundred metres.

```
   surface sensor  ▲            ▲  (surface sensor)
   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~│~~ shallow layer (top ~200 m) ~~
   borehole sensor ●            │
                    \           │
                     \  same deep path from the LFEs
                      ★ LFE family (~30 km down)
```

If surface S gets later compared with borehole S, the extra delay can only come from that top layer.

**5. What we saw.** After the Ferndale (2022) and M7 (2024) earthquakes, S at surface stations arrived about **2–2.5 ms later** than at the boreholes, then recovered within about a year.

**6. How big a change that is.** S needs about 0.3–0.5 s to cross the top ~200 m. An extra 2.5 ms in 0.4 s means that layer slowed by about 0.6%, roughly 0.5–1%. That's what shaking damage near the surface looks like: loosened soil and rock that slowly heals.

**7. Why we believe it:**

- It shows up at the quakes and not at quake-free dates.
- It survives removing every station with clock problems.
- The coda dv/v says the same: surface stations dropped 0.3–0.6% at the M7, boreholes about 0.1%.
- A deep change would delay distant stations more than nearby ones, and that pattern isn't there.

In short: a deep change would affect everything, and a shallow change only affects the sensors above it. The difference between sensors above and below the top layer isolates the shallow part, and that's where the quake changes show up.
