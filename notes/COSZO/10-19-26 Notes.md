# Seminar Notes: Cascadia Subduction Zone Seismology & Fault Mechanics

**Speaker:** Dr. Harold (Director of the Pacific Northwest Seismic Network, Paris Chair in Geohazards/Seismology)  
**Presentation Context:** Seminar presented at Marine Sciences Building 15 (previously delivered at _Seismo Lunch_ and _Tectonic Super Group_)  
**Key Collaborators:** Dr. Anna Leetsi (Columbia University / post-doc) and Dr. Maddie Lucas (Grant/Crant)  
**Core Thesis:** A proposed link between Cascadia's offshore geological structure, geodetic locking, and megathrust rupture dynamics explains why the Cascadia subduction zone behaves the way it does.

---

## 1. Subduction Zone Paradigms & Frictional Mechanics

- **Subduction Zone Architecture:** Subduction zone megathrusts form at the interface between an overriding continental plate and a subducting oceanic plate. The subducting plate brings down oceanic crust and overlying sediments into regimes of increasing temperature and pressure.
- **Frictional Regimes along the Plate Interface:**
    - **Seismogenic / Locked Zone:** Capable of frictional locking and storing elastic strain energy in surrounding rock mass. When strain exceeds frictional capacity, rapid slip occurs, releasing energy as earthquake rupture.
    - **Stable Regime:** Characterized by continuous, slow sliding (aseismic slip) without elastic strain accumulation.
    - **Unstable Regime:** Characterized by frictional locking that, upon failure, exhibits positive feedback (velocity weakening)—where friction decreases as slip speed increases, driving seismic rupture.
    - **Ductile Regime:** Located deep down-dip where high temperatures cause plastic deformation (flowing continuously like silly putty), preventing strain energy storage.
- **The Canonical Up-Dip Paradigm vs. Paradigm Shift:**
    - **Traditional View:** The up-dip margin and accretionary wedge were historically considered frictionally stable buffers incapable of storing elastic strain due to soft, unlithified sediments and low normal stress. Earthquake ruptures were thought to originate at 20–30 km depth and die out in this shallow buffer.
    - **Hazard Dichotomy:** Down-dip rupture produces strong ground shaking, whereas shallow up-dip rupture displaces the seafloor to generate tsunamis.
    - **Observational Gap:** Directly instrumenting the shallow offshore environment is difficult due to a lack of seafloor geodetic and seismological networks relative to onshore deployments.
    - **Global Paradigm Shift (Post-2011 Tohoku-Oki):** The 2011 \(M_w 9.0\) Tohoku-Oki earthquake demonstrated massive shallow slip exceeding **50 to 100 meters** all the way to the trench axis. Differential bathymetry and direct submersible observations (e.g., Victor Vescovo's submersible dives) confirmed large surface fault scarps. Ocean Drilling Program (ODP) core analyses in the Nankai Trough had previously hinted at co-seismic heating along shallow detachment faults.
    - **Global Analogues:** Shallow megathrust rupture has also occurred in major historical events including 2004 Sumatra, 1960 Chile, 1964 Alaska, and Kamchatka.

## 2. Tectonic Setting & Structural Framework of Cascadia

- **Thermal & Tectonic Boundary Conditions:**
    - **Young, Hot Subduction:** The Juan de Fuca plate subducts crust that is only ~9 million years old at the trench, making Cascadia an end-member "hot" subduction zone.
    - **Convergence Rate:** Accumulates potential slip at **40 mm/year** (~4 cm/year).
    - **Sediment Influx:** Merging fan systems (Astoria Fan and Nitinat Fan) deliver massive sediment volumes up to **3+ kilometers thick** at the deformation front off Washington and northern Oregon.
- **Offshore Fold-and-Thrust Belt:**
    - The accretionary prism forms a massive offshore fold-and-thrust belt approximately **100 km wide by 800 km long**, structurally comparable in scale to the northern Rocky Mountains.
    - Bathymetric imagery reveals north-south trending ridges, each representing an anticlinal fold overlying an active or inactive thrust fault.
    - Cross-sectional geometry off Grays Harbor shows an inner, older accretionary wedge bounded seaward by a rapidly built outer Pleistocene accretionary wedge (<2.5 Ma sediments).
- **Coulomb Wedge Mechanics & Splay Faulting:**
    - Accretionary wedges behave as Coulomb wedges (analogous to sand pushed ahead of a bulldozer blade).
    - Wedge geometry is governed by three primary variables: (1) basal fault friction, (2) internal material friction, and (3) pore fluid pressure.
    - **Splay Faults:** Branching faults that diverge from the main megathrust detachment upward through the wedge. Splay fault rupture efficiently redirects horizontal slip into steep vertical displacement, significantly amplifying tsunami hazard potential.

## 3. The Anomaly of Landward-Vergent Faulting in Cascadia

- **Landward-Vergent ("Wrong-Way") Fault Geometry:**
    - While standard accretionary wedges feature forward-breaking (seaward-vergent) thrust faults, central Cascadia (off northern Oregon, Washington, and southern Vancouver Island) is dominated by **landward-vergent** (backward-dipping) thrust faults.
    - This landward-vergent belt spans a region roughly **400 km long by 60 km wide** off Washington and northern Oregon.
    - Landward vergence is globally rare, observed only in select regions such as northern Sumatra, parts of the 1964 Alaska rupture zone off Kodiak Island, and a small patch on the Gorda plate off California.
- **Spatial Correlation with Geodetic Locking:**
    - Onshore GPS data alone cannot resolve offshore locking non-uniqueness (models range from zero offshore locking to full locking to the trench). However, recent GNSS-Acoustic offshore transponder data (John De Santo et al.) support strong up-dip locking toward the trench.
    - The geographic footprint of landward-vergent faulting coincides remarkably well with the zone of **maximum geodetic locking** (locking ratio \(= 1.0\)), where the upper plate is fully stuck to the subducting plate.

## 4. Physical & Frictional State of the Cascadia Megathrust Detachment

Evaluating physical conditions along the detachment fault (the _Tacoma_) shows that the shallow fault satisfies all conditions for frictional locking:

1. **High Normal Stress:** The low detachment dip combined with **2.5 to 3.0 km** of sediment overburden provides high ambient normal stress required for frictional locking.
2. **Elevated Temperatures:** Thermal modeling (Marie Salmi et al.) based on heat flow and gas hydrate proxies indicates detachment temperatures at the deformation front exceed **150°C to 170°C**. (In contrast, the Japan Trench detachment is cold, sitting at 20°C–40°C). High temperatures activate diagenetic and metamorphic processes (clay alteration, quartz/carbonate cementation).
3. **High Lithification & Seismic Velocity:** Pre-stack depth migration seismic inversions (Susanna Webb) demonstrate \(P\)-wave velocities exceeding **4,000 m/s** (\(>4.0\text{ km/s}\)) along the detachment. This matches onshore lithified rock outcrops and contrasts with weak, unlithified sediments (e.g., Nankai Trough drilling at ~2.5 km/s).
4. **Near-Hydrostatic Pore Fluid Pressure:** Seismic velocity analysis indicates the Cascadia outer wedge is well-drained, exhibiting minor overpressure (only **10–20% excess pore pressure**) rather than extreme fluid overpressurization (~90%).

**Conclusion:** High normal stress, elevated temperatures (\(>150^\circ\text{C}\)), high seismic velocities (\(>4\text{ km/s}\)), and low pore fluid pressures confirm the shallow detachment is **frictionally locked** during the interseismic period.

## 5. Geological Evidence for Active Outer-Wedge Rupture

- **Mapping Active vs. Inactive Fault Domains:**
    - High-resolution seismic reflection and Sparker profiling (Anna Leetsi, Janet Watt / USGS) across piggyback slope basins mapped structural activity across the margin.
    - Identified a **20 to 30 km wide active fault domain** in the outermost wedge, backed by an inactive domain further landward.
    - High-resolution bathymetry revealed **13-meter seafloor fault scarps** cutting modern sediments, proving recent co-seismic surface rupture on splay faults.
- **High-Resolution Seafloor Evidence in Southern Cascadia:**
    - Recent AUV 1-meter bathymetric lidar and AUV Chirp profiling off southern Oregon and Northern California (Janet Watt) show widespread surface-rupturing fault scarps along frontal thrusts.

## 6. The Mechanical Paradox & Dynamic Weakening

- **Mechanical Requirements for Landward Vergence:**
    - Mechanical Coulomb wedge modeling (Nadia Kubas energy balance method) demonstrates that landward-vergent faults can only form if the basal friction coefficient (\(\mu_{\text{base}}\)) is **exceptionally low** (\(\mu_{\text{base}} \le 0.03\), and potentially \(<0.01\)).
    - **The Material Paradox:** No natural geological material possesses static friction this low. Talc has a static friction coefficient of \(\sim 0.15\); even a banana skin sliding on a floor has a friction coefficient of \(\sim 0.07\)–\(0.10\)—twice as frictional as needed for Cascadia.
- **Resolution via High-Velocity Dynamic Weakening:**
    - Laboratory rock-friction experiments demonstrate that when rock surfaces slide at seismic velocities (\(\ge 1\text{ m/s}\)), dynamic thermal pressurization and pore fluid effects cause apparent friction to drop dramatically below **0.1** regardless of rock composition.
- **Dual-State Fault Behavior Model:**
    - **Interseismic State (~500 years):** The fault is statically strong, exhibiting high normal stress and near-hydrostatic pore pressure, causing complete frictional locking.
    - **Coseismic State (During Rupture):** As an earthquake propagates from depth into the shallow wedge at high speed (\(>1\text{ m/s}\)), dynamic weakening reduces basal friction to ultra-low levels (\(\mu < 0.03\)), favoring slip along landward-vergent thrust faults.
- **Macro-Scale Seismic Indicator:** The regional existence of the landward-vergent fold-and-thrust belt is itself a large-scale, structural signature requiring megathrust seismic rupture to form.

## 7. Tsunami Hazard & Rupture Scenario Implications

- **Earthquake Magnitude Scenarios (\(M_w 9.0\)):**
    - The 1700 Cascadia earthquake generated a Japanese "orphan tsunami" consistent with a \(M_w 8.7\)–\(9.2\) event.
    - Calculating seismic moment for **20 meters of coseismic slip** across the fully locked, landward-vergent offshore patch yields a **\(M_w 9.0\) earthquake**—demonstrating that an \(M_w 9.0\) event can occur on this offshore patch alone without requiring full margin-length rupture.
- **Comparison to Tsunami Earthquakes:**
    - The 2010 Mentawai (\(M_w 7.8\)) tsunami earthquake in Sumatra selectively ruptured the shallow outer wedge, producing disproportionately massive tsunamis; seismic profiles there reveal landward-vergent faulting.
- **Future Investigations:**
    - High-resolution sub-bottom profiling (SP29 mounted on R/V _Thompson_, _Healy_, and _Kilo Moana_) and sediment coring will target Holocene reflector disruption to establish detailed slip chronologies and recurrence intervals for individual splay faults.
