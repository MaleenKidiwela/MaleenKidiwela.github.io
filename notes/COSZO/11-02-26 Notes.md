## 1. Executive Context & Core Research Problem

### 1.1 The Paleoseismic Acceleration Paradox

In the Puget Sound region of Washington State, the empirical geological record of shallow crustal earthquakes exhibits a pronounced non-linear acceleration in seismic moment release over the post-glacial epoch (the last 15,000 years). Paleoseismic investigations reveal a prolonged quiet interval following deglaciation, followed by a dramatic uptick in earthquake activity over the last 3,000 to 4,000 years.

This observed acceleration poses a fundamental challenge to conventional seismic hazard assessment methodology. Standard frameworks, such as the United States Geological Survey (USGS) National Seismic Hazard Model (NSHM), operate under the baseline assumption of a time-independent, constant rate of moment release along active fault zones. When applied to Puget Sound crustal faults, a linear rate calibrated to recent seismicity fails to describe the 15,000-year paleoseismic history, predicting dozens of unobserved early post-glacial earthquakes.

The contrast between observed paleoseismic trends and standard hazard modeling assumptions is summarized below:

- **Observed Paleoseismic Pattern**: Early post-glacial quiescence (15,000 to 4,000 years BP) followed by a sharp late-Holocene acceleration in seismic moment release.
- **Standard Hazard Model Assumption**: A constant, time-independent linear rate of moment release across all time windows.
- **Resulting Modeling Conflict**: Extrapolating modern high rates linearly into the past over-predicts early Holocene earthquake counts by several hundred percent, contradicting high-preservation geological records.

### 1.2 Central Research Hypothesis

To resolve this discrepancy, the research presented in this lecture evaluates the primary physical hypothesis: **Glacial Isostatic Adjustment (GIA) driven by the retreat of the Cordilleran Ice Sheet temporarily suppressed fault activity across Puget Sound by altering the regional stress field.**

As the mantle slowly relaxed following ice unloading, horizontal compressive stresses decayed over thousands of years, keeping crustal faults pushed away from failure. In the late Holocene, as GIA-induced stress relaxation equilibrated, background tectonic loading resumed dominance, driving the apparent acceleration in local paleoseismicity. To rigorously test this mechanism, the research team is constructing a two-stage nested numerical modeling workflow linking 3D finite-element geomechanical models to rate-and-state boundary-element fault simulations.

## 2. Regional Tectonic & Seismotectonic Setting

### 2.1 Clockwise Block Rotation and Crustal Contraction

The Puget Sound region represents a complex, highly active compressional zone situated within the Cascadia forearc. While subduction of the Juan de Fuca plate drives megathrust hazard along the Pacific coast, inland crustal deformation is dominated by the northward migration and clockwise rotation of the Oregon Coastal Range (the Cascadia Oregon forearc block).

As this forearc block rotates clockwise, it impinges upon the rigid structural backstop of the Canadian Cordillera to the north. This tectonic collision induces approximately **5 millimeters per year (****5\text{ mm/yr}****)** of north-south crustal contraction across the Puget Sound basin.

The regional tectonic sequence operates through a three-stage mechanical chain:

1. **Clockwise Rotation**: The Oregon Coastal Range forearc block rotates clockwise relative to North America.
2. **Northward Impingement**: Rotational momentum drives the forearc block against the stationary Canadian Cordillera backstop.
3. **Crustal Contraction**: Transpressional shortening of ~5\text{ mm/yr} accumulates within the Puget Sound basin, creating an array of active crustal folds and faults.

### 2.2 Active Crustal Fault Systems & Population Exposure

In response to regional contraction, an extensive array of shallow, active crustal faults has developed across the Puget Sound lowland. These structures predominantly comprise east-west to northwest-southeast striking reverse, oblique-reverse, and strike-slip faults. Key structures analyzed in this study include:

- **Seattle Fault Zone**: A major east-west striking thrust fault system directly underlying metropolitan Seattle.
- **Olympia Fault**: A regional compressional structure bounding the southern Puget Sound basin.
- **Southern Whidbey Island Fault Zone (SWIF)**: A broad, northwest-striking oblique-reverse fault system extending across northern Puget Sound.
- **Saddle Mountains Fault**: An active crustal reverse fault situated in the southeastern Olympic Peninsula foothills.

Nearly **4 million inhabitants** reside directly above or adjacent to these active crustal structures. Although shallow crustal faults generate smaller maximum earthquake magnitudes (typically M_w\text{ 6.5--7.5}) compared to Cascadia subduction megathrust events (M_w\text{ 9.0}), their close proximity to dense urban infrastructure results in localized peak ground accelerations (PGA) that dominate regional seismic hazard maps.

## 3. The Paleoseismic Record vs. Steady-State Hazard Assumptions

### 3.1 Synthesis of Paleoseismic Data (Sherrard & Styron, 2024)

The empirical baseline for this research relies on a comprehensive paleoseismic compilation published by Brian Sherrard and Richard Styron in 2024. This dataset aggregates decades of field observations across Puget Sound crustal faults, integrating radiocarbon-dated trenching, coastal marsh subsidence records, marine terrace offsets, and landslide damming events.

The analytical workflow utilized by Sherrard and Styron to quantify regional moment release follows a sequential, multi-step translation from field observations to cumulative moment metrics:

1. **Field Evidence Extraction**: Measuring surface displacement offsets and rupture length across identified fault traces.
2. **Empirical Magnitude Scaling**: Applying fault scaling relations to construct probability density functions (PDFs) for moment magnitude (M_w) and event age.
3. **Cumulative Moment Synthesis**: Integrating magnitude PDFs across all regional faults to build a cumulative seismic moment release curve spanning 15,000 years BP to the present.

### 3.2 Evaluating the Preservation Bias Hypothesis

A key scientific question is whether the low count of early post-glacial earthquakes reflects a true physical suppression of seismicity or merely incomplete geological preservation. As discussed in the lecture, several lines of evidence strongly disfavor preservation bias:

1. **Landscape Reset**: Deglaciation at ~15,000 years BP provided a pristine geological slate, creating clear geomorphic markers (such as proglacial lake sediments and drumlinoid topography) that have remained stable.
2. **Continuous Geological Archives**: Coastal bogs, marshes, and lacustrine deposits across Puget Sound preserve uninterrupted stratigraphic records extending continuously back to the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM).
3. **Absence of Widespread Erosion**: The region has not experienced widespread, catastrophic erosional events over the Holocene that could erase 66 major surface-rupturing events.

Consequently, the non-linear inflection in the cumulative moment curve represents a real physical change in the regional earthquake rate rather than an artifact of missing data.

## 4. Geomechanical Mechanism: Glacial Isostatic Adjustment (GIA)

### 4.1 Ice Sheet Geometry & Deglaciation History

During the Last Glacial Maximum, the Puget Sound lobe of the Cordilleran Ice Sheet advanced southward between the Olympic Mountains and the Cascade Range. At its peak thickness (~18,000–15,000 years BP), the ice sheet buried modern-day Seattle beneath approximately **3,400 feet (~1,000 meters)** of glacial ice.

Rapid retreat of the Puget Sound lobe initiated around 15,000 years BP, leaving dramatic topographic drumlins, glacial erratics, and scoured valleys across the urban corridor.

### 4.2 Stress Relaxation Time Scales: Vertical vs. Horizontal

The removal of this massive surface load induces Glacial Isostatic Adjustment (GIA), characterized by two primary processes operating on distinct physical time scales:

- **Vertical Stress Equilibration**: Vertical stresses equilibrate rapidly as the elastic crust rebounds, leading to localized uplift beneath former ice centers and collapse of peripheral forebulges.
- **Horizontal Stress Relaxation**: Horizontal viscoelastic stresses within the lithosphere and sub-lithospheric mantle decay over much longer time scales, controlled by mantle viscosity and relaxation rates.

Analytical GIA models demonstrate that post-glacial stress perturbations at seismogenic depths (~20 km) reach magnitudes of **~1 megapascal (1 MPa)** in Cascadia, with even higher values beneath thicker sections of the ice sheet. Because horizontal stress relaxation persists over multiple thousands of years, GIA creates a prolonged transient stress field that interacts directly with background tectonic loading.

## 5. Two-Stage Computational Modeling Architecture

To quantify the interaction between GIA stress relaxation and fault rupture dynamics, the researchers established a nested, multi-physics numerical framework combining two specialized software packages.

```
Stage 1: PyLith (3D Finite Element Model)
  └── Computes full 3D stress & strain tensor evolution across lithosphere and viscoelastic mantle.
      │
      ▼ (Spatial & Temporal Stress Tensor Outputs)
Stage 2: QuakeDFN (3D Boundary Element Model)
  └── Maps stress tensors onto planar fault geometries and simulates rate-and-state friction slip.
      │
      ▼ (Synthetic Earthquake Catalogs)
Evaluation & Validation
  └── Compares simulated cumulative moment curves against Sherrard & Styron (2024) paleoseismic data.
```

### 5.1 Stage 1: Finite Element Load & Rheology Modeling (`PyLith`)

Stage 1 utilizes `PyLith`, an open-source finite-element code designed for dynamic and quasi-static tectonic modeling. `PyLith` calculates the complete 3D stress and strain tensor field across the entire lithospheric domain over a 28,000-year simulation window. It captures complex surface traction histories, 3D crustal structures, dipping fault geometries, and depth-dependent viscoelastic mantle rheologies.

### 5.2 Stage 2: Boundary Element Fault Cycle Modeling (`QuakeDFN`)

Because full finite-element codes like `PyLith` cannot efficiently simulate spontaneous, multi-cycle fault slip driven by non-linear friction laws, the stress tensors from Stage 1 are exported into `QuakeDFN`. `QuakeDFN` is a 3D boundary-element code that simulates spontaneous fault rupture events governed by empirical **rate-and-state friction laws**:

\tau = \sigma_n \left[ \mu_0 + a \ln\left(\frac{V}{V_0}\right) + b \ln\left(\frac{V_0 \theta}{D_c}\right) \right]

where \tau is shear stress, \sigma_n is effective normal stress, V is slip velocity, \theta is the friction state variable, a and b are constitutive friction parameters, and D_c is critical slip distance. `QuakeDFN` generates synthetic earthquake catalogs, allowing direct comparison between simulated moment release rates and empirical paleoseismic curves.

## 6. Stage 1 Parameterization: Rheology, Loading & Forebulge Paradox

### 6.1 Mesh Generation and 3D Rheological Structure

Using the meshing utility `Gmsh`, the team constructed a high-resolution 3D tetrahedral finite-element mesh encompassing the continental crust, oceanic slab, and underlying mantle. Material properties are parameterized across distinct structural zones:

- **Continental Crust**: Defined by specific density, compressional wave velocity (V_p), and shear wave velocity (Vs).
- **Upper Mantle Viscosity**: Parameterized across 10^{18} \text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s} to 10^{20} \text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}.
- **Lower Mantle Viscosity**: Parameterized between 10^{21} \text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s} and 10^{22} \text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}.
- **Mantle Wedge Structure**: Incorporates a low-viscosity wedge above the subducting slab (with estimates dropping down to 10^{15} \text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}) paired with a high-viscosity serpentinized elastic nose in the wedge corner.

### 6.2 The Glacial Forebulge Discrepancy Paradox

A major finding highlighted in the presentation is the **Glacial Forebulge Paradox**. Standard GIA models using nominal upper mantle viscosities produce a substantial peripheral uplift bulge (70 to 100 meters in amplitude) surrounding the ice sheet ~15,000 years BP. However, relative sea-level (RSL) data along the coasts of southern Washington, Oregon, and Northern California show no evidence of a collapsing forebulge, indicating flat or monotonic sea-level histories.

The fundamental trade-off between local subsidence fit and regional forebulge suppression is detailed below:

|   |   |   |   |
|---|---|---|---|
|Modeling Strategy|Puget Sound Subsidence Fit|Peripheral Forebulge Fit|Physical Mechanics / Trade-Off|
|**Nominal Upper Mantle Viscosity (****10^{18}****–****10^{19}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}****)**|Excellent Match|Fails (Produces 70–100 m bulge)|Matches local land movement, but over-predicts regional uplift bulge.|
|**Elevated Upper Mantle Viscosity (****10^{20}+\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}****)**|Fails (Under-predicts subsidence)|Good Match (Suppresses bulge)|Dampens regional forebulge amplitude, but severely degrades Puget Sound subsidence fit.|
|**Non-Newtonian Power-Law Rheology (****\dot{\epsilon} \propto \sigma^n****)**|Targeted High Subsidence|Suppressed Bulge|Strain-rate-dependent viscosity localizes deformation under high shear, suppressing low-strain forebulges.|
|**Mesh & Time Resolution Refinement**|Increased Subsidence|Minor Bulge Suppression|Finer tetrahedral discretization increases local subsidence amplitude, requiring ongoing re-calibration.|

### 6.3 Decoupling Background Tectonic Loading

Simulating the 5 mm/yr regional tectonic contraction within the GIA finite-element mesh presents severe boundary condition challenges. Applying a direct 5 mm/yr displacement push requires a domain extending several load lengths beyond the ice sheet, distorting the GIA deformation field. Consequently, the team is transitioning to a decoupled approach: modeling GIA stress relaxation and tectonic loading separately in Stage 1, then superimposing their stress tensors prior to Stage 2 friction modeling.

## 7. Stage 2 Fault Rupture Mechanics & Rate-and-State Friction Simulations

### 7.1 Coulomb Failure Stress (\Delta CFS) Evolution

Preliminary Stage 1 stress outputs were evaluated using the Coulomb Failure Stress change metric:

\Delta CFS = \Delta \tau + \mu \left( \Delta \sigma_n + \Delta P \right)

where \Delta \tau is shear stress change in the slip direction, \Delta \sigma_n is normal stress change, \mu is friction coefficient, and \Delta P is pore pressure change. Negative \Delta CFS rates indicate that a fault is being unclamped or driven further from failure.

For the Saddle Mountains Fault, GIA ice unloading produced negative \Delta CFS rates between **-2.5 \text{ kPa/yr}** **and** **-3.0 \text{ kPa/yr}** from the Last Glacial Maximum through ~3,000 years BP. This prolonged negative stressing rate confirms that ice retreat actively suppressed fault failure over thousands of years.

### 7.2 Fault Rupture Dynamics & Stress Drop Calibration

Stage 2 preliminary simulations in `QuakeDFN` mapped these stress histories onto planar representations of regional crustal faults. Key mechanical metrics evaluated include:

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|Metric Evaluated|Simulated Model Range|Target Benchmark & Validation Status|
|**Static Stress Drop**|0.1 to 10.0 MPa (Mean: 2.0–4.0 MPa)|**Validated**: Matches observational global intraplate ranges.|
|**Seattle Fault Event Stress Drops**|1.0 to 2.5 MPa per event|**Validated**: 3 discrete events generated over a 28,000-year window.|
|**Rupture Length / Width Ratio**|Full Fault Ruptures|**Needs Calibration**: Currently lacks localized spatial patchiness.|
|**Magnitude-Frequency Scaling**|Biased Toward Large Events|**Needs Calibration**: Non-compliant with Gutenberg-Richter b-value scaling.|

To address tuning challenges in Stage 2, ongoing work focuses on three primary model adjustments:

1. **Friction Parameter Tuning**: Adjusting constitutive rate-and-state values (a-b) and critical slip distance (D_c).
2. **Stochastic Initial Stress Fields**: Introducing randomized initial stress distributions across fault surfaces to prevent artificial full-fault ruptures.
3. **Geodetic Strain Rate Matching**: Calibrating energy release against modern geodetic strain measurements.

## 8. Seismic Hazard Implications & Ongoing Model Refinement

### 8.1 Re-evaluating Time-Independent Hazard Assumptions

The preliminary multi-stage model outputs successfully reproduce a non-linear inflection in cumulative moment release, capturing the transition from post-glacial suppression to late-Holocene acceleration.

A direct comparison between traditional hazard assumptions and GIA-influenced frameworks highlights key operational differences:

- **Traditional USGS NSHM Assumptions**: Assumes a constant, time-independent earthquake probability based on long-term average slip rates, ignoring lithospheric stress memory.
- **GIA-Influenced Hazard Framework**: Recognizes time-dependent fault stressing rates, accounting for post-glacial suppression phases followed by activeLate-Holocene acceleration.

If modern crustal seismicity in Puget Sound is actively accelerating as GIA stress suppression wanes, time-independent models like the NSHM may miscalculate modern seismic risk. Depending on whether mantle relaxation has fully equilibrated or remains in a transient recovery phase, modern earthquake probabilities could be significantly higher than long-term 15,000-year averages suggest.

### 8.2 Ongoing Modeling Roadmap

To deliver robust quantitative bounds for regional seismic hazard assessments, the research team is actively executing several computational refinements:

- **Resolution Scaling**: Expanding fine temporal and spatial mesh density across `PyLith` to ensure convergence of mantle flow fields.
- **Randomized Initial Stress Fields**: Implementing stochastic stress distribution inputs in `QuakeDFN` to prevent artificial full-fault ruptures.
- **Non-Uniform Tectonic Boundary Conditions**: Coupling 3D regional tectonic strain fields with GIA stress tensors.
- **Multi-Fault Interaction**: Simulating concurrent slip transfer across interacting fault networks (e.g., Seattle Fault interacting with SWIF and Olympia Fault) rather than isolated single-fault domains.
- **Uncertainty Propagation**: Presenting seismic hazard outputs as bounded probability envelopes across variable viscosity and ice load models, rather than relying on a single deterministic prediction.
