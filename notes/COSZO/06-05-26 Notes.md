Toward Permanent Fiber Sensing on the Cascadia Subduction Zone: Integrating Distributed Acoustic Sensing with the Cabled COSZO Seafloor Observatory off Central Oregon

Maleen Kidiwela, William Wilcock

Abstract

Long-term submarine observations are essential for understanding subduction zones because the slip of great earthquakes occurs offshore. This summer the Cascadia Offshore Subduction Zone Observatory (COSZO), funded by the US National Science Foundation, will add seismic and geodetic instruments to the Ocean Observatories Initiative Regional Cabled Array off Newport, Oregon. By connecting new science junction boxes to existing primary nodes on the slope and shelf, COSZO uses established cabled infrastructure to extend geophysical coverage across the megathrust without building a new cable system. Each site will host a buried broadband seismometer, a strong-motion accelerometer, a low-frequency hydrophone, a differential pressure gauge, and calibrated absolute pressure gauges, with ocean current meters at selected sites. The result is a critical mass of co-located seismic and geodetic sensors capable of resolving how megathrust locking transitions between the deformation front and the coastline and whether transient slip occurs.

The Regional Cabled Array has hosted three distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) experiments, demonstrating that single and multi-span fiber sensing can operate concurrently with observatory operations. Adding permanent fiber sensing would complement COSZO by providing dense, continuous strain measurements across an expanded footprint, bridging gaps between discrete instrument sites. Because each science junction box includes open ports and the project retains unspent funds, the observatory is designed to grow, and independent investigator-driven proposals can add high-rate sensors, borehole observatories, or alternative pressure and acoustic sensors to the suite. Integrating fiber sensing with this diverse network would enable investigations of repeating and very low frequency earthquakes, accretionary prism deformation, and the baseline slip behavior of the shallow megathrust, while strengthening efforts to prototype offshore earthquake and tsunami early warning. We present the COSZO design, its expansion options, and a path toward permanent optical sensing on the Cascadia margin.
