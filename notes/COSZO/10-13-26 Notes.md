## Talk 1: "Nokia Bell Labs Talk.m4a"

- **Speaker & Affiliation:** Mikael Mazur, Telecommunications Engineer and Optical Researcher at **Nokia Bell Labs**.
- **Core Theme:** Bridging optical telecommunications engineering with geophysics to scale **Distributed Acoustic Sensing (DAS)** from isolated deployments to a unified **global sensing grid** leveraging existing commercial fiber infrastructure.

## 1. Global Scale & Motivation

- **Seismic Network Evolution:** Over the past 125 years, global seismic stations evolved from early prototypes into an international network spanning roughly 10,000 active nodes, enabling global Earth modeling. However, scaling physical sensor deployments further faces physical and logistical plateaus.
- **Telecom Infrastructure Advantage:** Terrestrial telecommunication networks currently exceed **5 billion kilometers** of deployed active fiber, complemented by over **1.5 million kilometers** of active submarine cables that transport the vast majority of international internet traffic. Leveraging this pre-existing infrastructure provides orders-of-magnitude scaling opportunities for sensor density.
- **Network-Level Sensing:** While initiatives like the "Global DAS Month" demonstrated the power of federating independent DAS units into collective datasets, the ultimate engineering goal is accessing commercial optical layers directly without disrupting telecommunication traffic.

## 2. Physical Fundamentals of DAS

- **Strain Rate Measurement:** DAS tracks how relative fiber segments expand or contract over time by transmitting electromagnetic pulses and evaluating two-way optical time-of-flight, measuring dynamic strain rate ($d\epsilon/dt$).
- **Fiber Bragg Gratings vs. Standard Fiber:** Early optical sensing relied on inscribed Fiber Bragg Gratings (FBGs) that acted as semitransparent mirrors at specific intervals. In telecommunications, however, reflections are detrimental, and standard fibers contain no intentional gratings.
- **Rayleigh Backscattering ("Optical Fingerprint"):** Standard fiber sensing exploits intrinsic **Rayleigh backscattering**, caused by random, microscopic density fluctuations frozen into silica glass during fiber drawing. Although random in distribution, this microscopic spectral pattern remains static once frozen. Coherent optical pulses interrogate the fiber, recording returning phase and amplitude. Dynamic strains shift these scattering centers relative to one another, producing phase changes that demodulate into continuous strain rate waterfall plots.

## 3. Four Major Engineering Challenges & Solutions

- **Challenge 1: Coexistence on Live Telecom Fibers:**
    - _Mechanism of Interference:_ Introducing sensing signals onto active fibers governed by the **Nonlinear Schrödinger Equation** risks severe service disruption. When pulsed sensing channels co-propagate alongside Dense Wavelength Division Multiplexing (DWDM) data channels, rapid optical power fluctuations cause cross-phase modulation (XPM). This shifts the optical phase rapidly, rotating complex Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM) constellations (e.g., 16-QAM) and causing severe decoding bit errors.
    - _Solution:_ Replacing pulsed Optical Time-Domain Reflectometry (OTDR) with constant-power waveforms, such as **Optical Frequency Domain Reflectometry (OFDR)** or continuous chirped frequency pulses. Launching continuous-power chirped waveforms (even at high launch powers exceeding $+10\text{ dBm}$) causes zero measurable distortion or constellation rotation on co-propagating DWDM live channels.
- **Challenge 2: Dynamic Range, Clipping, and Gauge Length:**
    - _The Dilemma:_ System sensitivity is traditionally tied to channel spacing and physical gauge length. Long gauge lengths provide high Signal-to-Noise Ratios (SNR) for micro-earthquakes but saturate and clip during large tectonic events; short gauge lengths capture violent shaking but drown weak signals in the noise floor.
    - _Solution:_ Moving away from fixed instrument parameters by treating channel spacing and gauge length as free digital processing parameters derived from a single optical waveform. Field trials on the Kamaishi subsea cable in Japan demonstrated simultaneous extraction of multiple channel spacings. Current Bell Labs architectures interrogate **25 different channel spacings and gauge lengths in parallel** in real time from a single optical transmission, resolving weak ambient signals and intense shaking without clipping.
- **Challenge 3: High Data Volumes & Computational Architecture:**
    - _The Data Myth:_ A standard DAS system operating on a 100 km fiber with 1 m spatial resolution at 1 kHz generates approximately 100 million points per second, translating to $400\text{ MB/s}$ ($3.2\text{ Gb/s}$) or terabytes per day. While burdensome for local SSDs, this data rate is negligible compared to modern optical networks. Standard commercial transponders (such as Nokia Photonic Service Engines) routinely carry $1.2\text{ Tb/s}$ per metro wavelength and $800\text{ Gb/s}$ over subsea links; a single telecom fiber can carry up to 27 channels across a 4 THz C-band, handling the aggregate throughput of **6,875 concurrent high-rate DAS units**.
    - _Compute Architecture:_ Modern workstation GPUs (such as an Nvidia RTX 6000 Pro) offer $1.8\text{ TB/s}$ of internal memory bandwidth and hundreds of teraflops of compute. The true bottleneck is legacy CPU architectures that continuously shuffle data between disk, RAM, and processor registers. The path forward adopts the high-energy physics model of **CERN's Large Hadron Collider (LHC)**: streaming raw sensor data directly into accelerated GPU memory via remote memory mapping and applying real-time edge triggers to filter and extract high-value events.
- **Challenge 4: Overcoming Reach Limitations in Subsea Cables:**
    - _Subsea Constraints:_ Submarine cables present the greatest observation gap: while the median distance to a seismometer on land is ~150 km, the open ocean remains virtually unmonitored. Standard subsea links utilize unidirectional **Erbium-Doped Fiber Amplifiers (EDFAs)** in repeaters spaced every 50–80 km to prevent laser oscillation, traditionally halting DAS interrogation at the first subsea repeater.
    - _High-Loss Loopback (HLL) Couplers:_ Transoceanic repeater housings include high-loss loopback couplers (providing ~40 dB attenuation) designed solely for slow, static optical line monitoring and cable break localization.
    - _OFDR SNR Recovery:_ Standard pulsed OTDR on a 2,000 km subsea link operates with a microscopic duty cycle of roughly $1/2,000$ (transmitting for $10\text{ }\mu\text{s}$ and waiting empty for the remainder). Deploying continuous chirped OFDR injects optical energy continuously across time, recovering up to **33 dB of lost SNR** to compensate for the 40 dB coupler loss.
    - _California–Hawaii Ocean Trial:_ Installed on an unrepeatered 28-year-old, **4,400 km commercial transoceanic cable** between California and Hawaii. The system successfully monitored the entire seafloor, capturing seismic arrivals from the Kamchatka earthquake across the Pacific Basin. It also recorded the deep-ocean dispersion envelope of the accompanying tsunami across 4,400 virtual sensors down to frequencies of **$100\text{ }\mu\text{Hz}$**.
    - _Bidirectional Interrogation:_ Because optical signal loss escalates asymmetrically away from repeaters, launching interrogations simultaneously from both California and Hawaii shifted the lowest SNR point to the center of each span, achieving an additional **10 dB SNR enhancement**, a **20 dB noise reduction at 2 Hz**, a **50 m spatial resolution**, and a sensitivity of **$1\text{ n}\epsilon/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$** across 88,000 sensing channels.

## Talk 2: "W. M. Keck Observatory 2.m4a"

- **Speaker & Affiliation:** Chad Trabant, Data Facility Director at **EarthScope Consortium** (operating the NSF National Geophysical Facility).
- **Core Theme:** Cloud data architecture, standardized metadata, containerization, and cost sustainability models for managing massive DAS data volumes within public academic archives.

## 1. Facility Evolution & The DAS Data Ingestion Ban

- **Consortium Consolidation:** IRIS (seismology) and UNAVCO (geodesy) merged in 2023 to create EarthScope Consortium, which was formally awarded the 5-year NSF National Geophysical Facility (NGF) cooperative agreement in 2025.
- **The NSF Ban:** EarthScope operates three DAS interrogator units for community use, yet the National Science Foundation formally barred the facility from archiving raw DAS data. In 2023 alone, global DAS experiments produced more data than the entire volume accumulated across EarthScope's preceding 40-year historical seismic and geodetic archive. Without structural policy and infrastructure shifts, open archiving of raw DAS would overwhelm existing operational budgets.

## 2. Cloud Migration & In-Situ Processing Paradigm

- **Decommissioning On-Prem:** Two weeks prior to the talk, EarthScope finished removing the final hardware racks from its physical data centers, completing a full operational migration to the **Amazon Web Services (AWS)** commercial cloud.
- **"Compute Near the Data":** Ingesting, downloading, and moving hundreds of terabytes across campus networks is no longer sustainable. EarthScope is dismantling the legacy "download then read" paradigm in favor of "compute near the data," running computational workflows directly within the cloud object environment.
- **GeoLab Environment:** EarthScope deployed GeoLab, an in-cloud JupyterHub ecosystem co-located with the S3 data stores, accompanied by training libraries to help researchers transition desktop Python workflows into cloud-native execution without data transfers.
- **Machine Learning Cloud Benchmark:** Demonstrating the speed of in-cloud computing, a research group (Earle, You, et al.) analyzed roughly **1 Petabyte of MiniSEED data** from Northern and Southern California archives in AWS using the deep-learning picker PhaseNet. The team processed **4 billion seismic phase picks in under 72 hours**, incurring compute costs of \$15,000–\$20,000 (\$5,000 supported by EarthScope).

## 3. Cloud Object Storage Realities vs. POSIX Filesystems

- **Object Semantics vs. Disk:** Standard scientific codes written in Python, C, or Fortran assume a POSIX filesystem where arbitrary byte ranges can be read instantly with low latency. AWS S3 and cloud storage systems are object stores accessed via HTTP REST requests.
- **Access Pitfalls:** S3 is engineered for massive, parallel throughput of large contiguous byte streams. However, attempting rapid, microscopic seeks (e.g., fetching 10 bytes, seeking 5,000 bytes forward, and fetching another 10 bytes) creates catastrophic HTTP overhead and crippling latency. Tools like `fsspec` help bridge standard filesystem semantics to cloud storage.
- **The Problem with HDF5:** Although prevalent among commercial interrogator manufacturers, HDF5 is fundamentally misaligned with cloud object storage because it relies heavily on POSIX random read operations across file headers and chunks. Establishing an archive containing 15 disparate proprietary variations of HDF5 is unviable for a national data center.

## 4. International Standards: Metadata and Data Containers

- **FDSN Metadata Schema:** Beginning with the NSF DAS Research Coordination Network (RCN) in 2023, the community drafted the v1 DAS metadata schema in 2024. The International Federation of Digital Seismograph Networks (FDSN) adopted this draft, developed v2 through international working group reviews, and submitted it to the FDSN Steering Committee for formal adoption in late 2026. The schema includes dynamic extension mechanisms to adapt as optical hardware evolves.
- **Standardized DAS Data Container:** In February 2026, EarthScope convened with European, Australian, and New Zealand geophysical peers to define a standardized cloud-native DAS data container. The format must resolve two orthogonal data access patterns:
    1. _Long-term single-channel retrieval:_ Extracting continuous multi-year time series from 10 specific channels out of 10,000.
    2. _Broad spatial snapshot retrieval:_ Extracting a 2-minute time window across all 10,000 channels to generate a regional wavefield record section.
- **Vendor Adoption Pressure:** Because individual Principal Investigators (PIs) buy the vast majority of interrogators, EarthScope urged the community to demand that interrogator manufacturers natively output data into the emerging FDSN container standard.

## 5. Archival Sustainability & Cost Models

- **S3 Standard Realities:** Storing 5 TB generated in 6 days of recording costs ~\$80,000 annually if maintained perpetually on AWS S3 Standard. Because EarthScope aims to preserve national datasets across a 50-year horizon, alternative storage models are required:
    - _Option 1: Open Data Ring Buffer:_ Storing the most recent or high-impact raw datasets in the AWS Open Data Sponsorship Program, which provides free public storage and zero egress fees. Datasets circulate through a finite sliding buffer before rolling into cold deep storage. _Trade-off:_ AWS Open Data strictly prohibits authentication, making it impossible to enforce proprietary data embargoes or access restrictions for PIs.
    - _Option 2: Derivative Products:_ Storing high-utility compressed representations in active storage, such as spatially decimated channels (e.g., downsampling 7 m spacing to 100 m arrays), lossy spectral/numerical compression, or event-triggered time windows around eruptions and earthquakes.
    - _Option 3: Full Fidelity in AWS Glacier with "Requester-Pays":_ Archiving 100% of raw full-fidelity datasets in AWS Glacier tiers at ~\$1 per terabyte per month. When researchers require full raw datasets, retrieval requests require hours to stage data into S3, with the requester covering restoration and egress costs.

## 6. Institutional Examples & Q&A

- **Caltech DAS Hub:** A working model where institutional compute clusters host raw DAS datasets alongside Slurm batch managers, Jupyter tutorials, and 20–30 active research users without moving raw data.
- **Discussion on Data Embargoes:** Addressing questions on whether data restrictions will disappear, Trabant noted that while funding agencies increasingly push for immediate open data, eliminating embargo periods risks triggering the "shoebox effect"—where PIs withhold raw datasets on unmaintained local hard drives, resulting in permanent data loss.

## Talk 3: "W. M. Keck Observatory 3.m4a"

- **Speakers & Affiliations:**
    - Part 1: Dr. Helen Janiszewski, Assistant Professor of Geophysics at the **University of Hawaii at Mānoa**.
    - Part 2: Dr. Megan Holmes, Seismologist at the **Australian National University (ANU)** and Project Lead for the Mauna Kea DAS deployment.
- **Core Theme:** Hawaiian plume-plate volcanism, tectonic earthquake classifications, regional seismic network gaps, and the execution of the Mauna Kea dark fiber DAS experiment.

## 1. Hawaiian Volcanology & Magmatic Evolution (Dr. Helen Janiszewski)

- **Plume-Plate Framework:** The Hawaiian archipelago is generated by the Pacific tectonic plate moving northwestward over a buoyant, stationary mantle plume.
- **Four Systematic Volcanic Stages:**
    - _1. Pre-Shield Stage:_ Submarine proto-volcanoes located southeast of Hawaii, exemplified by Kamaʻehuakanaloa (formerly Lōʻihi Seamount).
    - _2. Shield-Building Stage:_ Represents the peak eruptive volume, tapping the hottest axial core of the mantle plume with the highest melt fractions. Characterized by high-frequency, voluminous effusive eruptions of tholeiitic basalt with low silica/alkali content and low viscosity. This produces broad, smooth shield edifices with shallow summit magma reservoirs (e.g., Kīlauea and Mauna Loa).
    - _3. Post-Shield Stage:_ As the volcano drifts away from the plume core, melt volume decreases and temperatures drop. Magma shifts to alkalic compositions enriched in sodium and potassium, increasing magma viscosity. This produces steeper slopes, rough cinder/scoria cones, and deepening magma plumbing systems (e.g., Mauna Kea and Hualālai). Cross-sections show Mauna Kea's post-shield alkalic cap resting atop a massive, older tholeiitic shield edifice built over Kohala's flanks.
    - _4. Rejuvenated Stage:_ Enigmatic eruptive activity occurring long after a volcano moves past the hotspot plume, characterized by isolated alkalic vents (e.g., Diamond Head / Lēʻahi on Oahu).

## 2. Seismicity & Tectonic Hazards on the Big Island

- **Hazard Profile:** The 2023 USGS National Seismic Hazard Model assigns the Island of Hawaii the highest seismic hazard rating in the United States, on par with the San Andreas Fault in California and the Cascadia Subduction Zone.
- **Seismogenic Regimes:**
    - _Volcanic/Magmatic Earthquakes:_ Shallow events ($<10\text{ km}$ depth) caused by subsurface magma transport, dike intrusions, and reservoir pressurization. Although essential for eruption forecasting, they are generally low in magnitude.
    - _Basal Décollement & Flank Earthquakes:_ Hawaii's largest earthquakes occur along a basal detachment zone where the volcanic volcanic pile rests atop pre-existing oceanic crust. Gravitational loading drives seaward flank slumping along low-angle thrusts, generating destructive events up to magnitude 7.9–8.0 (e.g., the 1868 Great Kaʻū earthquake) and triggering local tsunamis.
    - _Lithospheric Flexure Earthquakes:_ Deep earthquakes ($20\text{--}40\text{ km}$ depth) caused by the oceanic lithosphere bending downward under the enormous weight of the volcanic shield edifices. These occur along deep fault systems and can exceed magnitude 6.5 (e.g., the 2006 M6.7 Kīholo Bay earthquake offshore Kohala, which disrupted power across Oahu).
    - _Pāhala Seismic Swarm:_ A deep mantle seismogenic zone located at $\sim35\text{ km}$ depth beneath the southern flank. The region experienced a massive, decade-long surge with order-of-magnitude increases in seismicity between 2019 and 2022, characterized by persistent volcanic tremor and deep magma transport.
    - _Second Boiling CLVD Earthquakes:_ Mauna Kea's summit hosts unique deep earthquakes characterized by non-double-couple, Compensated Linear Vector Dipole (CLVD) focal mechanisms. Rather than active magma recharge, these events are driven by "second boiling"—the exsolution and release of high-pressure magmatic fluids as residual magma bodies undergo crystallization within the cooling post-shield system.
- **The Network Disparity:** Permanent networks (HVO, USGS, PTWC) and temporary PI deployments since 2000 are concentrated almost entirely on Kīlauea and Mauna Loa's active rift zones. Despite hosting major astronomical observatories, Mauna Kea's summit infrastructure has been historically monitored by only a single accelerometer and one vertical-component broadband sensor.

## 3. The Mauna Kea Dark Fiber Experiment (Dr. Megan Holmes)

- **Infrastructure Leverage:** Rather than undertaking complex environmental permitting to dig new installations on sacred and ecologically sensitive land, the project repurposed a **10.2 km dark fiber cable** owned by the University of Hawaii IT network.
- **Geometry and Layout:** The fiber runs from the Halepōhaku mid-level facility server room at **2,800 m elevation** up to the astronomical summit at **4,200 m elevation**, terminating near the W. M. Keck Observatory domes.
- **Tap Testing & Calibration:** Field crews hiked down the scree, cinder, and tuff slopes to perform manual tap tests at maintenance pits spaced roughly 35 m apart, discovering that the actual buried conduit path deviated substantially from official telecom utility maps.
- **Acquisition Setup:** Interrogated using a Silixa iDAS unit configured with a **10 m gauge length** and **4 m channel sampling**.
- **Observations to Date:** Since launching acquisition in July, the cable recorded over **1,500 local earthquakes**, including proximal Mauna Kea flank events (M1.7 and M3.0), deep Pāhala swarm clusters, vehicular traffic, and ambient hurricane noise variations.
- **Workshop Logistics & Q&A:** Distributed curated subsets of the Mauna Kea dataset across USB drives for workshop participants to run collaborative tutorials covering earthquake picking, localization, and noise interferometry. Holmes noted international logistics hurdles, including importing the Australian interrogator under a strict temporary ATA Carnet requiring customs re-export.

## Talk 4: "W. M. Keck Observatory 4.m4a"

- **Speakers & Affiliations:**
    - Part 1: Luke Bradley, Research Assistant and Optical/Electronic Engineer at **Monash University**.
    - Part 2: Prof. Arnan Mitchell, Director of the **TMOS Centre of Excellence** at **RMIT University / Melbourne**.
- **Core Theme:** Optoelectronic mechanics inside standard DAS interrogators, their fundamental physical limitations, and how integrated **silicon nitride optical microcombs** can overcome these boundaries.

## 1. Optoelectronic Architecture of a Standard DAS Interrogator (Luke Bradley)

- **The Interrogator Hardware Train:**
    1. _Ultra-Narrow-Linewidth Laser:_ Produces a continuous-wave reference light. Linewidth must be exceptionally narrow; laser phase wander over time directly mimics ground strain noise on the fiber.
    2. _Beam Splitting:_ The beam splits into a Local Oscillator (LO) reference arm and a pulse generation arm.
    3. _Acousto-Optic Modulator (AOM):_ Carves the continuous wave into short optical pulses while applying an RF frequency shift of 100–200 MHz.
    4. _Amplification & Routing:_ Pulses are boosted via an Erbium-Doped Fiber Amplifier (EDFA) and directed through an optical circulator into the sensing fiber.
    5. _Coherent Detection:_ A tiny fraction (~one-millionth, or $-60\text{ dB}$) of the pulse backscatters via Rayleigh impurities. The returning photons pass through the circulator and mix with the LO in a $2\times2$ optical coupler.
    6. _Balanced Photodetector (BPD):_ Detects the mixed optical signals, subtracting two $180^\circ$ out-of-phase outputs to eliminate common-mode laser intensity noise and generate a clean 200 MHz electrical beat signal, converting optical phase shifts into measurable electrical amplitudes.
    7. _IQ Demodulation:_ Mixes the 200 MHz detector output with an electronic RF reference matching the AOM drive frequency (one arm in-phase, one shifted $90^\circ$) to downconvert the signal to DC, yielding In-phase ($I$) and Quadrature ($Q$) signals.
    8. _Phase Extraction & Unwrapping:_ Phase is calculated via $\theta = \arctan(Q/I)$. Because arctangent is bounded within $[-\pi, +\pi]$, software phase-unwrapping algorithms must track and stitch $2\pi$ cycle jumps to recover continuous phase.
    9. _Strain Conversion:_ Taking the spatial phase difference between two points separated by a gauge length yields ground strain; evaluating phase differences across successive pulses yields strain rate ($d\epsilon/dt$).

## 2. Physical Limitations of Conventional Single-Wavelength DAS

- **Interference Fading:** Because coherent reflections from microscopic scatterers interfere constructively or destructively within a pulse width, roughly **1 in 10 sensing channels fades** below the noise floor at any given time, compounded by random polarization drift.
- **Laser 1/f Phase Noise:** Intrinsic laser phase noise escalates at low acoustic frequencies ($1/f$ behavior), obscuring sub-Hertz and millihertz signals critical for deep Earth tomographic imaging.
- **Nonlinear Power Thresholds:** Overcoming the 60 dB backscatter loss by boosting laser launch power triggers **Stimulated Brillouin Scattering (SBS)** and Kerr nonlinearities, generating acoustic phonons in the glass that destroy pulse coherence.
- **Dynamic Range & Phase Unwrapping Errors:** Strong seismic arrivals that rotate optical phase by more than $\pi$ between successive laser pulses cause phase-unwrapping algorithms to skip cycles, creating clipping and distortion on large-amplitude signals.
- **Data Deluges:** Raw $I/Q$ digitization at hundreds of megasamples per second generates upwards of **$86\text{ TB/day}$**, forcing interrogator manufacturers to apply hardware downsampling that discards raw signal data.

## 3. Optical Microcombs & Kerr Solitons (Prof. Arnan Mitchell)

- **Chip-Scale Micro-Rings:** Microcombs replace large optical benches with silicon nitride ($\text{Si}_3\text{N}_4$) chips featuring integrated optical waveguide ring cavities measuring roughly 1 mm in diameter.
- **Resonant Cavity Dynamics:** Coupling a 1-Watt pump laser into the micro-ring builds circulating optical powers to **kilowatt levels** inside the waveguide, accessing extreme third-order ($\chi^{(3)}$) optical nonlinearities safely within the chip.
- **Four-Wave Mixing to Solitons:** Resonant circulating light undergoes Four-Wave Mixing (FWM), spawning secondary equidistant wavelengths. As pump power rises, self-phase modulation (SPM) balances anomalous group velocity dispersion, collapsing chaotic modulation instability into highly stable **dissipative Kerr solitons**—spatially confined pulses of light circulating the ring.
- **Soliton Crystal States:** Rather than relying on fragile single-soliton states that cause steep optical power drops and thermal de-tuning, the team employs **soliton crystals**—densely packed, stable ensembles of solitons filling the cavity. Soliton crystals maintain steady thermal equilibrium, allowing deterministic, repeatable tuning into stable comb states.

## 4. Transformative Microcomb Opportunities in DAS (Luke Bradley & Arnan Mitchell)

- **Eliminating Fading via Multi-Wavelength Diversity:** Distributing interrogation across multiple mutually coherent comb lines circumvents interference fading. Because Rayleigh fading is wavelength-dependent, interrogating across 3 comb lines drops fading probability from 1 in 10 to 1 in 1,000; deploying dozens of comb lines effectively eliminates fading altogether.
- **Overcoming Brillouin Nonlinear Limits:** Distributing launch power across dozens of discrete comb frequencies keeps each individual line below the single-wavelength SBS nonlinear threshold, allowing dramatically higher aggregate optical energy to be injected into the fiber and extending reach (e.g., boosting unamplified reach from 43 km to 72 km using just 10 lines).
- **Common-Mode Laser Phase Noise Rejection:** Because all comb lines originate from a single pump and are phase-locked together, subtracting the phase of two comb lines (e.g., separated by 2 THz) completely cancels out common-mode laser phase noise, substantially lowering the low-frequency ($<1\text{ Hz}$) noise floor.
- **Dynamic Range Doubling for Violent Shaking:** Beating two comb lines separated by 2 THz lowers the effective carrier frequency relative to the nominal 193 THz optical band, reducing the rate of phase rotation between pulses during large strain events and increasing dynamic range by **~100% without degrading baseline sensitivity**.
- **All-Optical Signal Processing:** Microcomb lines can be manipulated directly in the optical domain to execute Hilbert transforms, analog optical bandpass filtering, and spatio-temporal dispersion delay lines. This allows interrogators to beamform and focus optical energy onto specific fiber zones (e.g., km 50 to 60), eliminating unwanted data collection and delivering real-time event detection.

## 5. Audience Q&A

- **Fading Statistics:** In response to audience questions regarding Maxwellian fading statistics and square-root-of-$N$ SNR scaling, Mitchell explained that because comb lines are coherently locked rather than independent, the comb can be actively shaped to ensure backscattered signals always sum constructively.
- **Brillouin & Temperature Decoupling:** In response to queries about separating thermal drift from tectonic strain, Mitchell noted microcombs can simultaneously interrogate Brillouin frequency shifts (~10 GHz) to measure absolute temperature while tracking Rayleigh phase for dynamic strain.
- **Manufacturing & Ruggedness:** Chip-scale microcombs leverage standard CMOS semiconductor fabrication found in consumer electronics, pointing toward future mass-manufactured, highly ruggedized interrogators capable of operating reliably in harsh field environments.

## Talk 5: "W. M. Keck Observatory.m4a"

- **Speaker & Affiliation:** Sara (referenced as the introductory volcanology and DAS speaker).
- **Core Theme:** Operational realities, field logistical challenges, and geophysical modeling methodologies of deploying DAS for seismicity monitoring, hazard characterization, and geodetic tracking on active volcanoes.

## 1. Fundamental Principles and Spatial Coherence of DAS

- **Phase-to-Deformation Sensing:** Laser pulses interrogate intrinsic Rayleigh scattering impurities along optical fibers; stretching or shortening of the glass induces phase shifts in returning backscatter proportional to axial strain. The technology achieves sub-nanometer-scale displacement sensitivity—equivalent to resolving a 4 cm change along a cable encircling the entire Earth equator.
- **Dense Spatial Arrays:** Repurposing a single 50 km optical fiber yields thousands of virtual seismic stations sampled every few meters (or sub-meter) at kilohertz frequencies.
- **Extracting Coherent Wavefields:** While single-channel seismograms from noisy volcanic environments often appear as uninterpretable random noise, multi-channel DAS arrays reveal spatially coherent wavefronts, enabling the detection of micro-events that cannot be identified on isolated stations. 2D color waterfall plots map wave moveout, velocity changes, and spatial attenuation across 1,500+ contiguous channels.

## 2. Fieldwork Logistics & Deployment Realities on Volcanoes

- **Interrogator Protection:** High-precision interrogators require stable power, thermal stability, and shelter from wind, rain, and corrosive gases. Deployments at established astronomical observatories or industrial power plants provide indoor shelter, whereas remote volcanoes require building specialized field shelters.
- **Self-Deployed Fibers vs. Commercial Dark Fibers:**
    - _Self-Deployed Installations:_
        - _Advantages:_ Full control over cable geometry, path selection, and ground coupling quality.
        - _Logistical Hazards:_ Extreme terrain, steep cliffs, and volcanic hazards restrict where personnel can lay cable. At Mount Meager in Canada, steep topography forced teams to transport equipment via helicopter onto an isolated ridge. At Grímsvötn and Askja calderas in Iceland, field teams used heavy snowplows weighted down with personnel standing on the blades to trench fiber cables into snow, ice, and volcanic soil.
    - _Existing Commercial / Telecom Dark Fibers:_
        - _Advantages:_ Highly cost-effective, eliminates installation hazards, and enables immediate crisis response. During the Reykjanes volcanic crisis in Iceland, researchers connected an interrogator inside the Svartsengi geothermal power plant, placing the instrument on a table beneath protective covering while earthquakes shook plaster from the ceiling. Existing cables also provide access to otherwise unreachable offshore submarine environments.
        - _Disadvantages:_
            1. _Poor Ground Coupling:_ Telecom fibers installed in loose conduits or subsea sediments often couple poorly to rock, producing incoherent, ringing waveforms that obscure primary ($P$-wave) travel time arrivals.
            2. _Geometric Degeneracy:_ Telecom cables often follow straight highway utility corridors. A perfectly straight fiber layout creates axial location symmetry, collapsing earthquake hypocenter inversions onto the cable axis and generating severe location artifacts.
            3. _Connector Incompatibilities:_ Commercial telecom providers utilize a wide variety of proprietary optical connectors, necessitating custom patch cables or hazardous on-site fusion splicing under field conditions.

## 3. Geophysical Analysis & Volcanic Case Studies

- **Event Detection & Environmental Signals:**
    - Coherent seismic arrivals display diagonal moveout trajectories that are readily distinguished from horizontal optical instrument noise bands.
    - Dense spatial sampling enables serendipitous discoveries of unsuspected physical phenomena: along a fiber traversing the ice sheet over Grímsvötn caldera in Iceland, DAS recorded continuous 4-second period resonance oscillations reflecting the hydrodynamic seiche of an active subglacial lake beneath the ice cap.
    - A comparative study using an onshore telecom fiber in Greece demonstrated that DAS successfully matched national earthquake catalog detections, missing only low-magnitude events at great epicentral distances.
- **Hypocenter Inversion & Geometry Trade-offs:**
    - Inverting DAS travel times accurately resolves horizontal earthquake epicenters along the array. However, because surface-laid fibers lack vertical aperture, hypocentral depth estimates suffer from significant trade-offs and uncertainties.
    - _Geometric Trade-off:_ Straight cable segments are ideal for ambient noise cross-correlation and wavefield integration, whereas multi-directional or curved segments are strictly required to triangulate earthquake hypocenters.
- **Full-Waveform Moment Tensor & Source Mechanism Inversion:**
    - At Grímsvötn volcano in Iceland, researchers executed full-waveform moment tensor inversions for sub-magnitude-1 volcanic earthquakes.
    - Achieving stable source mechanisms required comprehensive 3D numerical forward modeling incorporating irregular topography, ice-sheet interfaces, volcanic velocity structures, and directional DAS gauge length projections.
    - The resulting focal mechanisms resolved prominent non-double-couple (tensile/volumetric) components characteristic of hydrothermal fluid fracturing and magmatic injection.
- **Long-Term Geodetic Monitoring & Slow Deformation:**
    - In addition to high-frequency seismicity, low-frequency DAS phase trends can track slow, continuous crustal deformation.
    - During volcanic unrest at Svartsengi/Grindavík in Iceland, continuous DAS phase drift measured over weeks and months successfully tracked volcano-tectonic inflation and deflation cycles, showing strong concordance with co-located continuous GPS displacement time series.
