# COSZO Summer Internship Project Plan
## Huddle Testing of Ocean Bottom Seismometers Before Deployment off Newport, Oregon

*Cascadia Offshore Subduction Zone Observatory (COSZO)*

---

## 1. Project summary

The Cascadia subduction zone produces great M9 earthquakes roughly every 500 years on average. Between those events the offshore fault is mostly locked and quiet, but the few small earthquakes that do occur, plus many more that are too small to detect from land, may carry important clues about how the fault behaves. Late this summer the COSZO project will deploy four seafloor seismometers on the continental margin off Newport, Oregon to listen for that offshore seismicity.

Before any instrument goes in the water, we have to be confident it works. The standard way to check is a **huddle test**: we set all four seismometers side by side on a concrete floor so they all feel the same ground vibrations, record at the same time, and then compare what each one wrote down. If the instruments are healthy, their recordings should look nearly identical. Where they disagree, the disagreement tells us something is off, or tells us about each sensor's own internal noise floor.

In this project the student will (1) watch the seismometers run during testing and learn what the hardware does, (2) learn the computer skills to download and plot the recorded seismic data, and (3) analyze the signals to measure how similar the four instruments really are, and decide whether they are ready for deployment.

---

## 2. Context for the student

**Why Cascadia.** The Juan de Fuca plate is sliding beneath North America offshore the Pacific Northwest. The shallow part of that plate boundary is mostly stuck (locked) between the big earthquakes, which makes it hard to study directly. Offshore seismometers sit much closer to the action than land stations, so they can hear smaller events and locate them better. COSZO is part of the effort to build that offshore listening capability.

**What an ocean bottom seismometer (OBS) is.** Each instrument is a self contained package designed to sit on the seafloor for months. Inside it has:
- a **seismometer** (the sensor that converts ground motion into voltage), usually recording three components of motion (vertical, north, east),
- often a **pressure sensor or hydrophone** that records water pressure changes,
- a **digitizer** that turns the analog voltage into digital samples at a fixed sample rate,
- a **recorder and battery** package, and
- a **clock** that timestamps every sample. Clocks drift slowly, which matters a great deal when you later try to combine data from several instruments.

**What a huddle test checks.** When sensors are co-located on the same rigid floor, they all experience the same input ground motion. For matched, healthy sensors:
- their **waveforms** should overlay almost perfectly,
- their **frequency content** (spectra) should match,
- their **coherence** (a frequency by frequency measure of similarity) should be close to 1 in the band where the real ground motion is louder than each sensor's internal noise,
- the **relative gain** between any two should be near 1 and the **relative phase** near 0,
- their **timing** should agree.

Where coherence falls off, you are usually looking at each sensor's own self-noise rather than a real signal, and that band tells you the quietest motion each instrument can actually resolve.

## 7. The analysis toolbox

This section explains each comparison and what a healthy result looks like. Starter code is in Appendix A.

### 7.1 Time-domain comparison
Overlay the same component from all four sensors over an identical window. Matched sensors overlay closely. A **difference trace** (sensor A minus sensor B) should be small relative to either signal. A large difference, a flat line, or an inverted trace points to a problem (dead channel, wrong gain, reversed polarity).

### 7.2 Spectra and PSD/PPSD
A spectrum shows how energy is distributed across frequency. The **PSD** normalizes this for window length so sensors can be compared fairly. The **PPSD** accumulates many short PSDs into a probability density, which is the field-standard way to characterize a sensor's noise. Plotting PPSDs against the **Peterson New Low and New High Noise Models** shows whether each sensor sits inside the expected envelope and whether the four agree.

### 7.3 Coherence and cross-correlation
**Magnitude-squared coherence** runs from 0 to 1 and measures, frequency by frequency, how linearly related two recordings are. Co-located healthy sensors should have coherence near 1 in the band where the shared ground motion exceeds their self-noise, and coherence that falls toward 0 where self-noise takes over. **Cross-correlation** gives the time lag of best alignment, which is a direct check on timing and clock agreement: the peak should sit at zero lag (or a small, explainable offset).

### 7.4 Relative gain and phase
From the cross-spectrum and auto-spectrum of a pair you can estimate the transfer function from one sensor to the other. Its magnitude is the **relative gain** (target near 1) and its angle is the **relative phase** (target near 0). A systematic gain offset means one sensor's calibration is off; a phase ramp means a timing problem.

### 7.5 Self-noise via the three-channel method (advanced)
With three or more co-located sensors recording the same input, the **Sleeman et al. (2006)** three-channel method separates the common signal from each sensor's incoherent self-noise, yielding a self-noise PSD per sensor. This is more informative than a two-sensor coherence floor because it isolates each instrument rather than giving a combined bound. The math uses cross-spectra among the three channels. The student should use a community-vetted implementation and confirm results against the original paper rather than coding the index conventions from scratch on the first try. Compare each estimate against the Peterson NLNM to see how quiet each instrument is.

## 8. How to decide "are they the same?"

A clear, defensible bottom line should rest on several agreeing lines of evidence, not one plot:

- **Waveforms** overlay and difference traces are small.
- **PPSDs** sit close together and within the Peterson envelope.
- **Coherence** is near 1 across the shared-signal band for every pair.
- **Relative gain** is near 1 and **relative phase** near 0 for every pair.
- **Time lags** from cross-correlation are at or near zero.
- **Self-noise** estimates are comparable across sensors and near the NLNM.

If one sensor consistently stands apart across these tests, it is the outlier and should be flagged for inspection before deployment. The report should state this plainly and quantitatively (for example, the worst-case coherence, the largest gain departure, the largest time lag).

## Appendix A: Starter Python snippets

These are scaffolds to adapt, not finished code. Channel codes, file paths, and metadata sources should be set to the real huddle dataset.

**Environment setup**
```bash
conda create -n coszo python=3.11
conda activate coszo
conda install -c conda-forge obspy jupyterlab numpy scipy matplotlib pandas
```

**Read, trim, and remove response for one sensor**
```python
from obspy import read, read_inventory, UTCDateTime

st = read("sensor1/*.mseed")        # waveforms (counts)
inv = read_inventory("sensor1.xml") # StationXML with the response

t0 = UTCDateTime("2026-07-01T00:00:00")
t1 = t0 + 600                       # a 10-minute window
st.trim(t0, t1)

st.detrend("linear")
st.taper(0.05)
st.remove_response(inventory=inv, output="VEL")  # ground velocity, m/s
st.plot()
```

**Overlay all four vertical components**
```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

streams = {name: read(f"{name}/*.mseed") for name in ["s1", "s2", "s3", "s4"]}
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(11, 4))
for name, st in streams.items():
    tr = st.select(component="Z")[0].copy()
    tr.trim(t0, t1)
    ax.plot(tr.times(), tr.data, label=name, lw=0.7)
ax.set_xlabel("Time (s)")
ax.set_ylabel("Velocity (m/s)")
ax.legend()
plt.show()
```

**PPSD against the Peterson noise models**
```python
from obspy.signal import PPSD

tr = read("sensor1/*.mseed").select(component="Z")[0]
ppsd = PPSD(tr.stats, metadata=inv)
ppsd.add(read("sensor1/*.mseed"))
ppsd.plot(show_noise_models=True)   # overlays NLNM and NHNM
```

**Coherence between two sensors**
```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import coherence

fs = tr1.stats.sampling_rate
f, Cxy = coherence(tr1.data, tr2.data, fs=fs, nperseg=4096)

plt.semilogx(f, Cxy)
plt.xlabel("Frequency (Hz)")
plt.ylabel("Coherence")
plt.ylim(0, 1)
plt.show()
```

**Cross-correlation time lag**
```python
from obspy.signal.cross_correlation import correlate, xcorr_max

cc = correlate(tr1, tr2, shift=500)   # search +/- 500 samples
shift_samples, value = xcorr_max(cc)
lag_seconds = shift_samples / tr1.stats.sampling_rate
print(f"Best lag: {lag_seconds:.4f} s, peak correlation: {value:.3f}")
```

**Relative gain and phase from spectra**
```python
from scipy.signal import csd, welch

f, Pxy = csd(tr1.data, tr2.data, fs=fs, nperseg=4096)
f, Pxx = welch(tr1.data, fs=fs, nperseg=4096)

H = Pxy / Pxx              # transfer function, sensor1 -> sensor2
gain = np.abs(H)           # target near 1
phase = np.angle(H, deg=True)  # target near 0
```

## Appendix B: Glossary

- **Huddle test:** co-locating multiple sensors so they record the same input, to verify they agree.
- **OBS:** ocean bottom seismometer.
- **miniSEED:** standard binary format for seismic waveform data.
- **StationXML:** standard format for station metadata and instrument response.
- **Instrument response:** the frequency-dependent factor relating ground motion to recorded counts; removing it gives physical units.
- **PSD:** power spectral density, energy per frequency.
- **PPSD:** probabilistic power spectral density, a probability distribution of PSDs over time.
- **NLNM / NHNM:** Peterson New Low and New High Noise Models, the standard quiet and loud reference noise curves.
- **Coherence:** a 0 to 1 measure of how linearly related two signals are at each frequency.
- **Self-noise:** the noise a sensor generates internally, setting the floor of what it can resolve.
