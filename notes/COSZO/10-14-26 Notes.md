## Day 2 DAS Workshop

### Building Fiber-Optic Fault Zone Observatories

This presentation, delivered by Jimmy, focuses on transforming fiber-optic networks into long-term **fault zone observatories** using **Distributed Acoustic Sensing (DAS)** to conduct scalable, "Parkfield-style" earthquake science.

#### 1. Motivation and Background

- **The Parkfield Precedent**: The landmark Parkfield experiment on the San Andreas Fault deployed dense instrumentation (seismometers, GPS, creepmeters, magnetometers, tiltmeters, and a deep deviated borehole via SAFOD) that captured the 2004 magnitude 6.0 earthquake and its cycle. However, Parkfield's scientific insights face generalizability questions, and the setup was cost-prohibitive and difficult to maintain over decades.
- **The DAS Opportunity**: DAS offers ultra-dense spatial sampling that prevents spatial aliasing across narrow, complex fault zones. By repurposing pre-existing commercial telecommunications fiber, researchers can deploy multi-year arrays with minimal maintenance across major plate-boundary structures.

#### 2. Experimental Arrays

- **Garlock Fault Array (Ridgecrest South)**: Managed by Caltech, this 100 km long array features 10 m channel spacing between Ridgecrest and Barstow in Southern California. It traverses the **Garlock Fault**, an intracontinental strike-slip fault capable of producing \(\ge\) M7.5 earthquakes that remains significantly less understood than the San Andreas.
- **Northern California Array (USGS / Cal Poly Humboldt)**: Deployed across the southern Cascadia subduction zone near the Mendocino Transform Fault, focusing primarily on a 15 km segment (expanded to include additional segments).

#### 3. Methodologies and Scientific Inquiries

- **Upper Crustal Expression (Shallow Imaging)**:
    - An active-source experiment was conducted along the Garlock Fault using an in-situ truck-mounted piston source without deploying dedicated seismometers.
    - Active source data (higher frequency) were combined with ambient noise cross-correlations (lower frequency virtual sources) in a joint inversion.
    - The resulting shallow velocity model (<200 m depth) revealed a distinct **low-velocity damage zone (LVZ)** near the mapped fault traces, biased slightly southward, with a lateral velocity contrast across the fault (faster to the north, slower to the south).
- **Deep Seismogenic Fault Structure & Shallow Corrections**:
    - Because shallow near-surface heterogeneity heavily distorts deep seismic signals, the shallow model was used to calculate delay-time corrections, effectively "peeling back" the top 200 m.
    - Using a cluster of earthquakes east of the array that acted as a fan shot, arrival times picked via PhaseNet-DAS were evaluated against 1D Earth models.
    - Comparing depth-integrated delay times to the shallow model demonstrated that the LVZ amplitude in the raw earthquake data is fully accounted for by the top 200 m. **No significant low-velocity damage zone exists on the Garlock Fault below 200 m depth**, indicating a strong, healed, or undamaged fault at seismogenic depths. The cross-fault material velocity contrast also disappears below a few kilometers.
- **Deep Crustal and Moho Offsets (\(P_{m}P\) Processing)**:
    - To image the Moho boundary beneath the Garlock Fault, the author analyzed high-frequency (5 Hz) Moho-reflected \(P_{m}P\) secondary phases following direct P-waves.
    - Because scattering from local near-surface heterogeneities masks coherent secondary arrivals, an **autocorrelation technique** was implemented. Since P and \(P_{m}P\) pass through identical near-surface scatterers, autocorrelating the wavefield isolates a sharp peak corresponding to their differential travel time.
    - The reflection bounce points traversed the Garlock Fault, revealing a sharp crustal thickness step: the Moho is **36 km deep to the north and 32 km deep to the south**. This vertical 4 km offset occurs across a zone at most 3 km wide, demonstrating that the Garlock Fault penetrates into the upper mantle and placing tight bounds on lower crustal/upper mantle strength.
- **Megathrust Structure at Depth**:
    - Applying the autocorrelation method to Northern California intraslab earthquakes isolated high-frequency (5–10 Hz) transmitted \(P\)-to-\(S\) conversions (\(PS\)) off the subducting slab interface and slab Moho.
    - Inverting these picks revealed that the megathrust interface is **~1.5 km shallower** than mapped by the Slab2 model and exhibits kilometer-scale **interface roughness**, which directly affects slip mechanics.
- **Dynamic Rupture Tracking (2024 M7.0 Mendocino Earthquake)**:
    - The array recorded the 2024 M7.0 Mendocino earthquake on-scale without channel saturation.
    - Scattered surface waves were suppressed by converting strain data into particle velocity.
    - Moving-window **seismic beamforming** across the \(P\)-to-\(S\) wave window tracked the rupture front in space and time.
    - The beam azimuth rotated ~35° counter-clockwise, capturing an eastward rupture that initiated bilaterally, transitioned to unilateral propagation at ~3.5 km/s, paused for 1–2 seconds at the structural complexity of the Mendocino Triple Junction, and then accelerated into **supershear velocity**.

### Imaging Complex Fault Networks in the Brawley Seismic Zone

This talk presents the use of Imperial Valley dark fiber and earthquake scattering migration to map the hidden fault architecture linking the San Andreas and Imperial faults.

#### 1. Tectonic Background and Objectives

- **Brawley Seismic Zone (BSZ)**: Located in the Salton Trough between the Pacific and North American plates, this region accommodates active rifting, extension, and transtension. It contains major strike-slip faults including the Southern San Andreas Fault (SSAF), the Imperial Fault, and the San Jacinto Fault.
- **Structural Mystery & High Hazard**: The subsurface connection transferring slip between the SSAF and the Imperial Fault across the BSZ has remained unresolved due to a lack of surface expressions. The area experiences intense seismicity, historical M4+ events, and frequent earthquake swarms.
- **Geothermal Significance**: The region hosts one of the largest geothermal reservoirs in the United States (~3,000 MW capacity), characterized by extreme heat flow reaching up to ~300°C at 1,000 m depth.

#### 2. Array and Dataset

- **Imperial Valley Dark Fiber**: A 27 km commercial telecommunications fiber providing ~7,000 DAS channels.
- Recorded continuously for approximately 1.5 years during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022).
- While originally deployed for geothermal reservoir monitoring (using ambient noise to map \(V_p/V_s\) and build a 3D tomographic velocity model), the array recorded **over 5,000 local earthquakes**.

#### 3. Method: Fault Imaging via Earthquake Scattering Migration

- **Scattered Phase Identification**: Thousands of earthquake records exhibited prominent, coherent secondary arrivals trailing direct S-waves, identified as seismic energy scattered by subsurface fault damage zones.
- **Event Selection**: Over 300 earthquakes with high signal-to-noise ratios and clear scattering arrivals were selected for structural imaging.
- **Migration Workflow**:
    1. Applied frequency-wavenumber (\(f\)-\(k\)) filtering to extract the scattered wavefield while muting direct arrivals and near-surface multiples.
    2. Performed single-event **Kirchhoff migration** based on travel times through the regional 3D velocity model.
    3. Stacked the 300+ migrated single-event images to suppress migration artifacts and generate a 3D **fault reflectivity volume**.

#### 4. Key Findings and Geological Interpretation

- **Four Subsurface Fault Branches**: High-reflectivity zones resolved four major structural trends:
    - A **southern branch (red)** aligning directly with the northern projection of the Imperial Fault.
    - A **northern branch (orange)** extending the SSAF southward directly into the Salton Sea.
    - **Conjugate cross-faults (purple)** bridging the offset between the two main right-lateral systems, consistent with block rotation models of step-over pull-apart basins.
- **3D Fault Planes & Velocity Boundaries**: Slicing the reflectivity volume at successive depths produced 3D fault geometries that coincide with sharp lateral velocity contrasts identified in the 3D tomographic model.
- **Imaging Aseismic Structures**: The scattering approach mapped fault surfaces in regions lacking recent earthquake hypocenters, highlighting its ability to illuminate locked or aseismic segments.
- **Limitations**: Constraining fault dip angles remains challenging due to an asymmetric distribution of sources (earthquakes were clustered primarily to the west of the array, generating migration artifacts). Areas lacking reflected energy indicate a lack of illuminating sources rather than a confirmed absence of faults.

### Northern California DAS Testbed — EEW, Source Physics, and Coupling Constraints

This talk details a multi-institution testbed (USGS, Cal Poly Humboldt, Caltech) evaluating DAS for **Earthquake Early Warning (EEW)**, source characterization, overriding plate hazard assessment, and optical fiber coupling limitations.

#### 1. Testbed Overview and Objectives

- **Regional Setting**: Focuses on the southern Cascadia subduction zone and Mendocino Triple Junction in Northern California, an area with high offshore megathrust seismicity, complex plate coupling, and overriding crustal faults running directly beneath populated communities.
- **Arrays**:
    - An original 15 km fiber deployed in 2023 with five months of collocated **nodal seismometers**.
    - An expanded regional array traversing **Highway 299** across major active Quaternary structures.
    - Openly released continuous and event-based catalogs containing thousands of earthquakes from microseismicity up to M7.0.

#### 2. Source Physics and Earthquake Early Warning (EEW)

- **Sensor Validation**: Peak strain from DAS channels scaled consistently with peak ground velocity measured by collocated nodal seismometers (proportional to apparent wave slowness), showing strong waveform similarity.
- **Local Magnitude (\(M_L\)) Scaling**: Amplitude-based magnitude estimation matched catalog reference magnitudes from micro-earthquakes up to M5.4+. Achieving this agreement required calibrating and removing **channel-to-channel amplification and attenuation site response factors**.
- **Moment Rate Functions**: Far-field P- and S-wave strain records scale with moment acceleration. Using empirical Green's function (EGF) spectral ratios, moment rates and source durations matched nearby broadband seismometers, although strict channel quality screening and fiber azimuth corrections (E-W vs. N-S segments) were required.
- **Real-Time Rupture Growth for EEW**: Moving-window beamforming (building on Jimmy's work) combined with Wells and Coppersmith empirical scaling laws enables rapid tracking of offshore rupture length growth in real time, offering potential enhancements for systems like ShakeAlert before damaging waves reach coastal populations.
- **Focal Mechanisms**: \(S/P\) amplitude ratios from DAS scaled across orders of magnitude with nodal seismometer ratios; spatial gradients in strain across fiber bends provide tight constraints on nodal planes even when focal sphere coverage is limited.

#### 3. Overriding Faults and Basin Hazard Characterization

- **Active Fault Imaging**: Applying a back-projection migration to scattered wave energy (chevron arrivals) across the Highway 299 array successfully localized active Quaternary fault zones, including the **Mad River Fault Zone** (which accommodates ~1 cm/yr of plate motion with a history of M7 events).
- **Basin Edge Amplification**: High-resolution shallow velocity models delineated sharp basin boundaries that generate severe localized ground motion amplification, providing direct empirical constraints for hazard modeling parameters such as \(Z_{1.0}\) (depth to the 1.0 km/s shear-wave contour) and \(V_{S30}\).

#### 4. Critical Challenge: Cable Coupling and Waveform Coherence Degradation

- **Coherence Breakdown Under Strong Shaking**: During events causing moderate to strong shaking (Modified Mercalli Intensity IV to VI), including the 2024 M7.0 Mendocino earthquake, neighboring channels separated by only a few meters recorded wildly inconsistent strain waveforms and apparent "static offsets".
- **Magnitude-Dependent Reliability**:
    - During smaller events (e.g., an M5.3 aftershock), inter-channel coherence remained high throughout the P-wave, S-wave, and coda across all frequencies.
    - During the M7.0 mainshock, inter-channel coherence collapsed around the S-wave arrival and into the coda.
- **Operational Implication**: Because commercial telecommunications cables are installed in conduits rather than directly cemented to bedrock, strong dynamic strains appear to cause cable slip or imperfect ground coupling. Consequently, algorithms validated only on small or moderate earthquakes cannot be assumed to function reliably during large, damaging events.

### Hawaiian Rainforest.m4a: Volcanic Deformation Monitoring via Dark Fiber Seismology in Iceland

#### 1. Experimental Background and Deployment Setup

- **Regional Setting and Monitoring Objective:** The experiment monitored volcanic deformation on the **Reykjanes Peninsula in Iceland** using distributed acoustic sensing (**DAS**) deployed on **8 km of dark fiber** located directly above an inferred magma reservoir and adjacent to active eruption sites.
- **Rapid Deployment Design:** The monitoring campaign was launched rapidly in response to an intense seismic crisis. Instrumentation was optimized for real-time streaming to the local meteorological office, prioritizing lower sampling rates, wide channel spacing, and reduced raw data volumes.
- **Volcanic and Tectonic Activity:** Over the five-month deployment, the region experienced **four volcanic eruptions**, one non-eruptive dyking intrusion, severe ground deformation, and extensive seismicity.

#### 2. Observations: Seismicity and Ground Deformation

- **Seismicity Patterns:** Earthquakes clustered heavily along the active dyke system, with prominent seismic bursts preceding each volcanic eruption, followed by a substantial drop in seismic activity after the fourth eruption.
- **Regional Deformation Scale:** Co-located continuous **GPS stations recorded up to 0.5 meters of ground deformation** across the peninsula during the study window.
- **Low-Frequency DAS Transients:** Downsampling raw fiber recordings revealed distinct **low-frequency strain rate transients** occurring both immediately prior to and following eruptive phases.

#### 3. Signal Processing: Converting Strain Rate to Displacement

- **Temporal Integration:** Low-frequency strain rate records were integrated over time to derive continuous strain signals.
- **Spatial Integration:** Strain was subsequently integrated spatially along straight segments of the fiber to estimate **relative displacement**.
- **Constraints on Absolute Amplitude:** Because integration constants were unknown and no reference seismometer was located directly on the fiber trace, absolute displacement could not be recovered; however, the relative displacement orders of magnitude closely matched regional GPS measurements (~0.5 m).

#### 4. Comparison Between DAS and GPS Measurements

- **Instrument Differences:** GPS records absolute three-dimensional point position, whereas DAS measures along-fiber strain rate or relative axial displacement (extension and shortening), remaining insensitive to uniform rigid-body translation of the cable.
- **Differential Benchmarking:** To compare measurements, researchers calculated the differential displacement (east component) between two GPS stations bounding the fiber segment.
- **Discrepancies:** While broad deformation trends correlated, the GPS clearly registered each eruption, whereas DAS showed attenuated responses. This discrepancy is attributed either to the low-pass filtering effect inherent in spatial integration or potential physical maintenance work conducted on the fiber line.

#### 5. Analytical Source Inversion (Mogi Model)

- **Model Formulation:** Subsurface volume changes were inverted using a classical **Mogi point-source model** (a spherical pressure source inside a homogeneous elastic half-space).
- **Parameter Reduction:** By combining source overpressure and spherical radius into a single parameter (**volume change \(\Delta V\)**), the inversion was simplified to **four parameters** (\(x, y, z, \Delta V\)) solved via non-linear least squares across sequential time slices.
- **Okada Dislocation Comparison:** Attempts to invert rectangular Okada dislocation sources introduced nine parameters, which severely over-parameterized the problem and could not be adequately constrained by the fiber data.
- **Spatial Distribution:** Inverted single Mogi sources successfully captured first-order deformation trends and clustered near the fiber line and fault trace. However, a single source could not simultaneously resolve intense localized deformation at the distal end of the fiber alongside proximal signals, indicating source complexity.

#### 6. Daily Inversions and the Unexplained March Signal

- **Daily Resolution:** Day-to-day synthetic waveform inversions matched observed DAS deformation profiles with high fidelity, though detrending was necessary to remove large long-term instrumentation drift.
- **Periodic Signal Detection:** Beginning in March, a distinct, **approximately hourly cyclical signal** appeared in the recordings.
- **Source Characteristics:** Synthetic forward modeling reproduced this pattern through cyclic volume fluctuations of a localized source. The signal exhibited amplitudes too large to be anthropogenic, had irregular onset/termination times, and was localized directly beneath the southern part of the nearby town. This location was spatially distinct from northern civil-defense earthen barriers constructed to divert lava flows.
- **Complementary Synergy:** DAS cannot replace GPS due to its lack of absolute reference and baseline drift; however, DAS offers superior spatial and temporal resolution capable of tracking hourly subsurface volcanic volume shifts.

### Holo Holo Market 4.m4a: Physics-Constrained Low-Frequency DAS Inversion in Geothermal Systems

#### 1. Background and Subsurface Challenges in EGS

- **Research Context:** Presented by researchers from Stanford University’s Department of Geophysics in collaboration with Fervo Energy.
- **Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS):** EGS projects hydraulically fracture low-permeability crystalline rock reservoirs to establish permeable pathways for circulating fluid and extracting heat.
- **Operational Hazards:** Key reservoir challenges include **induced seismicity**, fluid loss, thermal drawdown at production wells, casing shear along intersected fault planes, and flow non-uniformity caused by preferential fracture pathways.

#### 2. Limitations of Conventional Monitoring and Unconstrained Inversions

- **Aseismic and Quasi-Static Deformation:** Hydraulic fracture opening and injection-induced fault slip represent slow, quasi-static, or aseismic processes that radiate little high-frequency seismic energy, leaving them largely undetected by conventional downhole or surface seismometers.
- **Potential of Low-Frequency DAS:** Low-frequency DAS recordings can capture quasi-static strain changes associated with tensile fracture opening (as previously demonstrated in magmatic dyke propagation).
- **Ill-Posed Inverse Problem:** Standard DAS inversions are severely underconstrained and non-unique because the number of optical sensing channels is far smaller than the spatial discretization points across the fracture plane. Furthermore, unconstrained inversions often generate unphysical opening geometries that violate momentum conservation or fracture mechanics principles.

#### 3. Physics-Informed Joint Inversion Framework

Stanford developed a multi-physics inversion incorporating explicit mechanical regularizations by jointly solving three coupled systems:

1. **Elastic Rock Deformation:** Simulates the strain field recorded by low-frequency optical fiber cemented behind well casing.
2. **Poroelastic Pressure Coupling:** Tensile fracture opening compresses surrounding formation rock, creating a pore pressure transient recorded by a downhole gauge installed at the injection depth in the monitoring well. Due to ultra-low crystalline reservoir permeability over short injection intervals, the process is treated as undrained.
3. **Mass Conservation:** Fluid leak-off into the rock matrix is neglected, strictly enforcing that total injected fluid volume equals total cumulative fracture volume.

- **Level-Set Fracture Representation:** A **level-set method** based on fracture front arrival times defines a compact, contiguous fracture geometry anchored at the injection perforation, encoding propagation history and stress heterogeneity.
- **Fracture Mechanics Asymptotics:** Opening displacement follows crack-tip asymptotic solutions governed by power-law exponent \(\alpha\) (derived from the governing propagation regime), with opening amplitude scaled to match net injected volume.

#### 4. Synthetic Validation and Non-Uniqueness

- **Synthetic Benchmark:** A hydraulic fracturing simulator modeled a laterally elongated crack propagating between higher normal stress barriers located above and below the injection perforation.
- **Multi-Fiber Configuration:** An inversion utilizing both vertical and horizontal fiber arrays successfully recovered the elongated fracture aspect ratio.
- **Single Vertical Fiber Limitations:** When restricted to a single vertical monitoring well, the inversion could not directly resolve horizontal crack elongation. However, it still estimated a length greater than height because the vertical fiber array bounded vertical propagation height, forcing the volume conservation constraint to distribute remaining volume horizontally. Both scenarios fit data residuals equally well, demonstrating inherent non-uniqueness.

#### 5. Field Application and Model Comparison (Fervo Energy EGS, Utah)

- **Field Setup:** Data was acquired during stimulation of horizontal wells at an EGS site in Utah, monitored by a vertical well equipped with cemented fiber and a downhole pressure gauge at treatment depth.
- **Stage Selection:** Geomechanical modeling revealed that early injection stages (Stages 8–11) corresponded to clean hydraulic fracture opening, whereas later stages involved complex fault activation and permeability evolution; inversion focused on Stage 12 hydraulic fracturing.
- **Unconstrained vs. Constrained Results:**
    - **Unconstrained Inversion:** Produced highly irregular, non-compact opening geometries with unphysical lateral fingers, while violating mass conservation (predicted fracture volume exceeded total injected fluid volume).
    - **Physics-Constrained Inversion:** Preserved mass balance and resolved a compact, **radially symmetric (circular) fracture expanding to roughly 200 meters in radius**.

#### 6. Reservoir Engineering and Geophysical Implications

- **Reinterpreting Microseismicity:** Injection-induced microseismic clouds extended **1 to 2 kilometers away** from the well. Reservoir engineers typically interpret these seismic clouds as evidence of 1–2 km long fracture growth. The physics-constrained DAS inversion demonstrated that the geomechanical data is entirely consistent with a compact ~200 m radius crack, proving distal microseismic events are triggered by **pore pressure diffusion along pre-existing fault networks** rather than physical fracture growth.
- **Geothermal Heat Sweeping:** Unlike layered sedimentary shale reservoirs that favor lateral fracture containment, homogeneous crystalline basement rock in EGS promotes radially symmetric growth. Vertical expansion drives fractures upward into shallower, colder rock while intersecting fewer lateral production wells, reducing thermal efficiency.
- **Broader Applications:** The constrained low-frequency DAS framework provides a tool for reducing non-uniqueness in ill-posed geomechanical inversions and is slated for tectonic fault slip applications (e.g., monitoring near the San Andreas Fault at the SAFOD borehole).
